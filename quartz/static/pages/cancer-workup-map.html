Who gets screened, and how

Screening is for people without symptoms at average risk. Anyone with symptoms, a past cancer or a high-risk history leaves the program and gets investigated or followed differently.

Why PSA is controversial

Test 1000 men: 102 will have prostate cancer, 10 may benefit from treatment, 1 will escape death.

PSA also rises with infection, trauma, sexual activity and DRE. Active surveillance is an option.

Prevent and screen a precursor

Many cancers start as precursor lesions. HPV vaccination prevents the HPV infection, and the Pap test screens for cervical pre-cancer, before squamous cell carcinoma of the cervix develops.

From lesion to pathology report

The basement membrane is the line between pre-malignant and malignant. The report then tells you what it is, how far it has spread (stage) and how it looks under the microscope (grade).

DysplasiaPre-malignant. Abnormal growth, maturation and nuclei. Only seen under a microscope
Carcinoma in situPre-malignant (high grade dysplasia). Full epithelial thickness, pushing on an intact basement membrane
Malignant neoplasmDysplastic cells have broken past the basement membrane
MetastasisCancer cells spread to and invade other tissues

Chart 5

Could this cancer be hereditary?

Most cancer is sporadic. Take at least 3 generations on both sides: cancer types, age at diagnosis, new primary versus metastatic.

History
Look for the red flags in the family historyEarly onset (breast < 50, ovarian < 60) · multiple primaries in one person · many relatives on the same side over generations · the overall pattern · ethnic background (BRCA1/2 in Ashkenazi Jewish families)

No pattern

Sporadic (70 to 85%)

Mutation in a single somatic cell. Not passed to children

Clustering, no clear inheritance

Familial (10 to 20%)

1st degree relatives have 2 to 3× population risk. Shared genes and environment

Red flags present

Hereditary (5 to 10%)

Mutation in every cell. Mendelian inheritance (autosomal dominant or recessive)

Consider referral ↓

Refer when
Genetic counsellingPersonal or family Hx of cancer, a test exists and can be interpreted, results may change management, and the patient wants to know

Test an affected relative firstConfirm the mutation in an affected family member before testing unaffected ones. Avoid testing children for adult-onset conditions

BenefitsClearer risk, earlier detection, relieves uncertainty, guides risk management, lets relatives choose testing
DrawbacksFalse positives and negatives, tests miss some genes, no good prevention for some cancers, some relatives do not want to know, more worry and tests

Chart 6

How cancer develops, and what causes it

Cancer is a cascade of mutations, rarely one. Most never progress far enough to be found clinically.

InitiationA genetic alteration makes a premalignant cell
PromotionPremalignant cells proliferate
TransformationA second rapid alteration makes a malignant cell
ProgressionFrom malignant cell to clinical cancer
Ask
Which kind of gene is mutated?Two-hit hypothesis: 1st hit is hereditary or a rare event, 2nd hit causes cancer

Gain of function, one hit

Proto-oncogene → oncogene

What
Normal growth gene that, once mutated, can transform a cell. One allele is enough
Examples
VEGF, EGFR, Ras, c-myc, Her2-neu, RET (medullary thyroid)

Loss of function, two hits

Tumour suppressor gene

What
Normally slows the cell cycle or promotes apoptosis. Both alleles lost. Higher risk with a positive FHx
Examples
p53, Rb, PTEN, BRCA1/2

The numbers

Cancer is the #1 cause of death in Canada. 1 in 2 Canadians will develop cancer and 1 in 4 will die of it, with 600 diagnosed per day. Canada incidence: lung, breast (#1 in women), colorectum, prostate (#1 in men), bladder. Canada mortality: lung, colorectum, pancreas, breast, prostate.
