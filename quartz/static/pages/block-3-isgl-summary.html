Imposter syndrome

Explain it, see how it affects your career, spot it and reduce it, and know the supports.

What it is

The persistent belief that you don't deserve your success and will be exposed as a fraud, despite clear evidence of competence.

  • An inaccurate self-assessment, closely tied to perfectionism.
  • First described by Clance and Imes (1978) in high-achieving women who credited their success to luck.
  • About 70% of men and women feel it at some point.
  • Triggers in medicine: high-stakes evaluation, errors, poor performance, moving into a new role.
The imposter cycle
  1. Task
  2. Anxiety
  3. Over-prepare or procrastinate
  4. Success
  5. Credit goes to luck or effort
  6. Praise discounted
  7. Fraud feeling grows, repeat

Success never fixes it, because the credit always goes somewhere other than ability.

Effects on your career
  • Over-preparing leads to burnout.
  • Hiding uncertainty and not asking for help puts patient safety at risk.
  • Turning down leadership roles or competitive specialties.
  • Responding to errors with shame instead of learning.
  • Weaker teaching, with no healthy uncertainty modelled for learners.
  • Higher risk of anxiety and depression.
Helping a classmate

Signs: goes quiet in small group, skips social events, puts themselves down, deflects compliments, gets anxious over low-stakes tasks, looks exhausted.

  1. Check in privately and casually.
  2. Listen first. Don't rush to fix it.
  3. Normalize it by sharing your own self-doubt.
  4. Name the pattern and point to evidence of their competence.
  5. Point them to supports. Escalate to the Learner Experience Office (LEO) or a faculty member for signs of depression or safety concerns.
Supports
Students
Schulich Learner Experience Office (free, confidential counselling), Western Wellness, mentors, peer support.
Residents Outside the module
PARO 24/7 helpline, program wellness leads.
Physicians Outside the module
OMA Physician Health Program, CMA Wellness Hub, employee assistance programs, their own family physician.

Implicit bias and SDH

Reduce the impact of bias, use the social determinants of health (SDH) and financial supports to plan care, and lower barriers for underrepresented groups.

Implicit bias

Unconscious attitudes or stereotypes that shape decisions, often against your stated beliefs. Having bias doesn't make you racist or sexist. It is a normal brain shortcut.

System 1
Fast, automatic, associative. Steers most behaviour.
System 2
Slow and deliberate.
  • Strongest under fatigue, stress, hunger, time pressure and heavy cognitive load.
  • Explicit attitudes have improved over decades. Implicit attitudes have not.
  • It feeds self-fulfilling prophecies: expecting mistrust makes you act guarded, which creates mistrust.
Implicit Association Test (IAT)
  • A timed sorting task that is hard to fake.
  • 75 to 80% of white Americans, providers included, show implicit anti-Black bias.
  • Predicts behaviour better than self-report. It has been criticized, so treat a result as a pointer to blind spots, not a verdict.
How bias shows up in care

CPSO examples

  • Women are 3 times less likely to be referred for knee replacement when it is indicated.
  • Black and Hispanic patients get less pain medication, at lower doses, despite higher pain scores.
  • Suicidal ideation is undertreated in older patients, though men aged 85 to 89 are at high risk.
  • COPD is underdiagnosed in women.
  • Lesbian and bisexual women get fewer timely Pap tests.

In the visit

  • Shorter visits, less warmth, faster speech, more verbal dominance.
  • Patients sense it, so trust, adherence and follow-up drop. The physician often thinks the visit went well.
  • Differential attribution: an out-group patient is "non-adherent", an in-group patient "forgot the timing".
Reducing bias

van Ryn's 3 strategies

Perspective-taking
Picture the visit from the patient's side, then check: "If I were in your shoes I might feel X. Am I close?"
Partnership-building
Use "we" and "let's", and name shared goals to create a common in-group.
Protect cognitive resources
Sleep, eat, manage stress, and fix system pressures like scheduling and staffing.

From the readings

  • Sukhera: normalize bias, practise self-compassion, use humility.
  • Capatosto: seek counter-stereotypic contact with groups you hold bias toward.
Social determinants of health

The social and economic conditions that shape health outcomes, mostly outside the health system.

In the case (Andrea, a PSW and single mother): income, employment and working conditions, gender and caregiving, access to health services, social supports, and health behaviours such as smoking.

Money and coverage Outside the module

Ontario government pages, checked Nov 4, 2026.

WSIB
Work injuries. Covers wages, drugs and physio. Ask if the problem started at work.
EI
Regular benefits, or sickness benefits.
Ontario Works (OW)
Basic financial assistance.
ODSP
Disability income support, with drug and physio coverage.
Seniors
CPP retirement, OAS and GIS. CPP Disability for disability.
Ontario Drug Benefit
Age 65 and over, under 25 without a private plan (OHIP+), OW or ODSP, long-term care, home care, Trillium enrollees.
Trillium
High drug costs relative to income. Income-based deductible.
OHIP physio
Age 19 and under, 65 and over, a hospital stay or day surgery in the last 12 months, or OW or ODSP. No referral needed.

Fit the plan to the patient: ask about coverage, cost, time off, transportation and childcare before they leave.

Underrepresented groups Outside the module
Newcomer, limited English
Professional interpreter, not family. Plain language and teach-back. Refugee claimants are covered by the Interim Federal Health Program (IFHP).
Opioid use disorder
Avoid diagnostic overshadowing and take the pain seriously. Non-opioid care first. Check the Narcotics Monitoring System. Offer opioid agonist therapy and naloxone.
Schizophrenia or bipolar
Avoid diagnostic overshadowing and do the full workup. NSAIDs raise lithium levels. Ask about ODSP. Involve the case worker with consent.
Older adult with MCI
Presume capacity and speak to them directly. Get consent before involving family. Avoid oral NSAIDs. Screen for falls.

Environment and health

Link environmental and climate factors to illness, spot vulnerable patients, and recommend choices that help both patient and planet. Linked MCC objective: Health and the Climate Crisis/Environment.

Sarah's case
  • 21 years old. Fever and right groin swelling for 1 week.
  • T 38.6, HR 115, BP 96/60.
  • Tender, soft, rubbery 1 to 2 cm groin nodes. No neck or armpit nodes. Possible mild splenomegaly.
  • A 5 to 6 cm circular red rash on the back of her upper leg.
  • Camped at Pinery Provincial Park 2 weeks ago. Worked in a chemistry lab over the summer.
  • Father had colon cancer in his early 50s. Mother has lupus.
Reading the case Outside the module
  • Lyme disease. A circular rash after camping, with fever, fits erythema migrans.
  • Climate link. Pinery is a known tick area in Ontario, and warmer temperatures expand tick range.
  • Reportable. Lyme is a disease of public health significance, so you report it to public health.
  • Differential for fever with nodes: mono, cat scratch disease, STIs (syphilis, HSV, LGV), lymphoma, local skin infection.
  • Distractor. The lab solvents don't explain her illness, but the module still asks you to discuss them.
What the module asks you to discuss
  • How heat and temperature shifts drive vector-borne disease.
  • How UV, herbicides, pesticides and solvents affect health.
  • Personal climate resilience.
  • Cutting the footprint of hospitals and clinics, and advocacy.

Readings: McCally (environment and health), Galanis and Ogden (climate change and emerging disease), Chekuri and Harper (climate-smart family practice), Clapp (environment and cancer).
