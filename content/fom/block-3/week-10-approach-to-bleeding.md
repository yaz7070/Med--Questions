## Hemostasis

- Bleeding: to emit or lose blood
- Hemostasis: a complex process in which multiple components of the blood clotting system are activated in response to vessel
injury to control bleeding
1. · Primary hemostasis = vasoconstriction and platelet plug formation
  - Triggered by injury to the vessel wall, exposing subendothelial collagen
  - Vasoconstriction: occurs at the site of injury to reduce blood flow – reflex neurogenic mechanisms
  - Adhesion: von Willebrand factor (vWF) adheres platelets to exposed subendothelial collagen via the platelet receptor
glycoprotein Ib/IX (GPIb/IX); platelets also adhere directly to collagen via other receptors
  - Aggregation: platelets aggregate with each other with the help of fibrinogen that binds to activated glycoprotein
IIb/IIIa (GPIIb/IIIa), forming a platelet plug
  - Contraction: activation of platelet leads to shape change (discoid à spheroid); secretion of platelet granular content,
influx of calcium; platelet plug provides negatively charged phospholipid surface for coagulation factor assembly
    - Platelets à arachidonic acid – cyclooxygenase à endoperoxides – thromboxane synthase à thromboxane A2
2. · Secondary hemostasis = activation of coagulation factors and generation of thrombin/fibrin
  - Initiation phase
    - Tissue factor (TF) is released from injured tissue cells, endothelial cells, and monocytes
    - TF and Factor VIIa form the TF/Factor VIIa complex
    - TF/Factor VIIa activates a small amount of Factor IX and X to generate a small amount of thrombin
    - Factor XII (and other “contact” factors) play a minor role in the activation of Factor XI
  - Amplification phase
    - Thrombin activates Factor V to Va, Factor VIII to VIIIa and activates more platelets
    - Thrombin also activates FXI to FXIa
  - Propagation phase
    - Additional Factor Xa is produced when TF/Factor VIIa complex activates Factor IX; the resultant Factor IXa
along with Factor VIIIa forms the tenase complex which then converts more Factor X to Xa
    - Factor Xa and Va along with calcium and a phospholipid (PL) surface (activated platelets) form the
prothrombinase complex which converts prothrombin (Factor II) to large amounts of thrombin (Factor IIa)

3. · Fibrin clot formation and stabilization
  - Thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin monomers which polymerize to form a soluble clot
  - Thrombin activates Factor XIII which cross-links the fibrin monomers and stabilizes the clot
4. · Inhibition of coagulation = inhibition of thrombin generation and fibrin clot breakdown (fibrinolysis)
  - Tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) converts plasminogen to plasmin – breaks down cross-linked fibrin to D-dimers
  - Thrombin activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI) inhibits the formation of plasmin and suppresses fibrinolysis
  - Anti-plasmin and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) inhibit plasmin and t-PA

## Sample Collection for Coagulation Testing

- To assess coagulation “in vitro,” the laboratory measures the time taken to form a clot
- Blood is collected into a blue top tube containing sodium citrate anticoagulant (which chelates calcium) to prevent blood
clotting in the tube during transport
- Plasma is separated from the platelets (phospholipid source) by centrifugation

## Routine Coagulation Tests and Scores

- Prothrombin time (PT): used to assess deficiencies or inhibitors of the extrinsic pathway factors and common pathway factors
  - PT reagent (0.1 mL thromboplastin + phospholipids + calcium chloride) + plasma (0.05 mL); incubate at 37°C
  - Normal = 9-15 seconds à can be normal in mild factor deficiencies
- International normalized ratio (INR): developed to standardize the PT to allow for monitoring of oral vitamin K antagonist
therapy across different labs; PT in seconds is used to calculate the INR
  - Normal = 0.9-1.1
- Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT): used to assess deficiencies or inhibitors of the intrinsic pathway factors and
common pathway factors
  - APTT reagent (0.1 mL contact activator + phospholipids) + plasma (0.1 mL) + CaCI2 (0.1 mL); incubate at 37°C
  - No tissue factor
  - Normal = 25-35 seconds à can be normal in mild factor deficiencies
- Thrombin time (TT): used to assess deficiencies or dysfunction of fibrinogen or the presence of an inhibitor of thrombin
  - Thrombin reagent (0.1 mL) + diluted plasma (0.2 mL); incubate at 37°C
  - Time in seconds is inversely proportional to fibrinogen activity – obtained from a standard calibration curve
  - Longer clotting time = lower concentration of fibrinogen in the sample
- Fibrinogen assay: assesses fibrinogen activity
  - Hypofibrinogenemia: loss of fibrinogen (bleeding), consumption (hyperfibrinolysis after traumatic injury, DIC),
decreased production (severe liver disease), congenital, dysfibrinogenemia (abnormal fibrinogen)
  - Fibrinogen is an acute phase reactant and may be non-specifically elevated with acute or chronic inflammation
- D-dimer tests: qualitative (positive or negative), quantitative (Enzyme Linked Immunosorbent Assay or Latex Immunoassay
which use a monoclonal antibody to various epitopes of D-dimer)
  - D-dimers: breakdown products generated by the action of plasmin on cross-linked fibrin
  - Negative D-dimer – used to rule out VTE
  - Elevated D-dimer – not specific to thrombosis; may be associated with other non-specific diseases or inflammatory
states (recent surgery/trauma, cancer, acute/chronic infectious/inflammatory diseases, DIC, elderly, pregnancy)
- Anti-Xa assay: used to measure the anticoagulant activity of an anticoagulant that inhibits clotting Factor Xa such as heparin,
LMWH, fondaparinux or direct Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban)
- 50:50 mixing study: combine 1 part patient plasma with 1 part normal plasma to differentiate a factor deficiency or inhibitor
  - Correction = factor deficiency; does not correct = inhibitor; partial correction = multiple factor deficiencies or inhibitor
- Well’s Criteria for DVT: active cancer; paralysis, paresis, or recent immobilization of lower extremities; recently bedridden > 3
days or major surgery within 4 weeks; localized tenderness along the distribution of the deep venous system; entire leg swollen;
calf swelling 3 cm over the other side (measured 10 cm below tibial tuberosity); pitting edema confined to symptomatic leg;
collateral superficial veins; no alternative diagnosis; previous history of DVT

- Onset of bleeding – spontaneous or with hemostatic challenges (dental extractions, surgery, postpartum, vaccinations)
- Location of bleeding – mucosal more likely primary, internal/deep more likely secondary
- Pattern of bleeding – bruises, petechiae, hematomas
- Duration, severity, and cause of bleeding episode
- Menstrual history
- Treatments/interventions required to stop bleeding – local pressure, cautery/packing for nosebleeds, other interventions
- History or symptoms of anemia/iron deficiency – fatigue, prior iron supplementation
- Previous blood transfusions
- Medication history – antiplatelets, anticoagulants
- Family history of bleeding problems – vWD, Hemophilia A/B, HHT, DIC, vitamin K deficiency; ethnicity, consanguinity
- Liver disease, kidney disease
Standardized bleeding assessment tools (BATs) should be used to assess bleeding risk
- Improve diagnostic accuracy, describe symptom severity, allow for effective communication, predict bleeding risk
- MCMDM-1 VWD: -1 to +4, 5-10 mins
- ISTH BAT: 0 to +4, 20 mins; 13 realms of bleeding; normal score &lt; 4 in adult males, &lt; 6 in adult females, &lt; 3 in children
- Pediatric bleeding questionnaire (PBQ): -1 to 4; positive score > 2
Lack of family history does not rule out a congenital bleeding disorder
PEx · Petechia &lt; 2 mm; purpura = 3-5 mm; ecchymosis > 5 mm
Skin – pallor, jaundice, size and location of bruises, hematomas, telangiectasia

Joints – range of motion, evidence of hypermobility, bleeding (coagulation factor deficiency)
Temperature – hypothermia promotes bleeding

factor deficiencies, collagen vascular disorders (Ehlers Danlos Syndrome), hypo/dysfibrinogenemia

## Blood Bank Tests

- Blood group: a serological test to determine the patient’s ABO blood group and their RhD blood group
  - Universal donor = O– (can be given to all other blood ABO/Rh recipients)
    - O+ if female > 45 or male; O– if female &lt;45
  - Universal recipient = AB+
- Antibody screen: a serological test to determine if the patient’s plasma contains any antibodies against red cell antigens
  - Antibodies may result in acute or delayed hemolysis; can form from past exposure to transfusion or pregnancy
  - Universal donor = AB plasma (but A plasma is also an option)
- Crossmatch: a serological recipient-donor compatibility test to ensure the patient’s plasma does not agglutinate the donor RBCs

## Platelets

- Derived from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow – shed platelets as cytoplasmic fragments
- Internal structure is divided into 4 zones

| Zone | Structure |
|---|---|
| Peripheral zone | Surface membrane – rich in glycoproteins on external surface (adhesion, aggregation); phospholipids (source of Thromboxane A2) |

Open canalicular system – transports substances into and out of platelets
Sol-gel zone · Under peripheral zone
Cytoskeleton – important for retaining and changing shape during activation
Organelle zone · Mitochondria, lysosomes
Contains granules which store critical signalling compounds
-Alpha (adhesive proteins) – fibrinogen, vWF, FV, FVIII, FXI, FXIII, HMWK, PAI-1
-Delta (dense for activation) – ATP, ADP (attaches to P2Y12 receptor), calcium, histamine, serotonin, epinephrine
Membrane system · Calcium store
Enzymes for prostaglandin synthesis

- Role: formation of hemostatic plug; provide phospholipid membrane surface for assembly of key coagulation proteins
- Anti-platelet drugs: clopidogrel (blocks P2Y12 receptor pathway), aspirin (inhibits cyclooxygenase; decreased thromboxane A2),
abciximab (directed against glycoprotein IIb/IIIa; prevents platelet aggregation)

## Coagulopathy in Adults

- Mild-moderate phenotypes of congenital bleeding disorders – severe is often identified earlier in life
- Congenital deficiencies with clinico-laboratory discrepancy – low levels of factors may not confer a severe bleeding phenotype
- Acquired bleeding disorders – Hemophilia A, vWD, platelet disorders

## Coagulopathy in Children

- Maturation of the hemostatic system over the first 6 months of life
- Immaturity of the liver results in lower levels of most factors – vitamin K dependent factors, protein C, protein S
- Physiologically longer PT and aPTT in premature infants and neonates compared to adults

## Approach to Thrombocytopenia

1. Thrombocytopenia = decreased platelets; &lt; 150 × 109/L – easy bleeding/bruising (mucocutaneous, epistaxis, peri-operative, menstrual, peripartum)
2. Assess severity – platelet count 100-150 = mild, 50-99 = moderate, 10-49 = severe (risk of bleeding with trauma/surgery), &lt; 10 = very severe (risk of spontaneous bleeding)
3. Consider the DDx (causes) – CBC and differential, peripheral blood film, nutritional deficiency workup, PT/INR, aPPT, fibrinogen, LFT, abdominal ultrasound
4. Consider associated disorders and related diseases
5. Put it all together for management

## Venous Thromboembolism

- Venous thromboembolism: a common medical condition that includes deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism
- 75% are spontaneous; 30% occur in cancer patients; 10-20% are due to surgery/trauma/hospitalization
- Highest risk = hip fracture or joint arthroplasty
- Virchow’s triad: three factors that are thought to contribute to thrombosis – stasis, injury, hypercoagulability
- Prevention of VTE: early mobilization, dalteparin/warfarin/fondaparinux/rivaroxaban/apixaban/edoxaban as long as the patient
is not ambulatory (minimum 7-10 days after surgery or 4-6 weeks if cancer or past Hx of VTE)

## Thrombophilia

- Thrombophilia: a group of inherited or acquired disorders that increase a person’s risk of developing thrombosis (blood clots)
- Indications for thrombophilia screen: idiopathic DVT/PE, young (&lt; 55), recurrent events, FHx, unusual location (brain,
mesentery, upper limb)
