## Week 9: Imposter syndrome

**Session objectives**

- Explain the concept of imposter syndrome.
- Describe its relevance to your career.
- Describe approaches to identify and minimize its effects.
- List the supports available.

**What it is**

- The persistent belief that you don't deserve your success and will be exposed as a fraud, despite clear evidence of competence.
- An inaccurate self-assessment, closely tied to perfectionism.
- First described by Clance and Imes (1978) in high-achieving women who credited their success to luck, not ability.
- About 70% of men and women feel it at some point.

**Triggers in medicine**

- High-stakes evaluation
- Errors and poor performance
- Transitions to new roles

**The imposter cycle**

1. A task comes up.
2. Anxiety.
3. Over-preparing or procrastinating.
4. Success.
5. Credit goes to luck or effort, not ability.
6. Praise gets discounted.
7. The fraud feeling grows, and the cycle restarts with the next task.

**Consequences for your career**

- Over-preparing leads to burnout.
- Hiding uncertainty and not asking for help puts patient safety at risk.
- Turning down leadership roles or competitive specialties.
- Responding to errors with shame instead of learning.
- Weaker teaching, and failing to model healthy uncertainty for learners.
- Higher risk of anxiety and depression.

**Spotting it in a peer**

Goes quiet in small group, skips social events, puts themselves down, deflects compliments, gets anxious over low-stakes tasks, looks exhausted.

**How to help**

1. Check in privately and casually.
2. Listen first. Don't rush to fix it.
3. Normalize it by sharing your own self-doubt.
4. Name the pattern and point to evidence of their competence.
5. Point them to supports. Escalate to the Learner Experience Office (LEO) or a faculty member if you see signs of depression or safety concerns.

**Supports**

- Students: Schulich Learner Experience Office (free, confidential counselling), Western Wellness, mentors, peer support.
- Residents: PARO 24/7 helpline, program wellness leads.
- Physicians: OMA Physician Health Program, CMA Wellness Hub, employee assistance programs, their own family physician.

## Week 10: Implicit bias and social determinants of health

**Session objectives**

- Review implicit bias, and ways to reduce its impact on care.
- Identify common social determinants of health (SDH) and how they inform health care navigation.
- Review financial support resources for patients, and how they inform care planning.
- Discuss ways to care for underrepresented populations and reduce barriers to care.

**Implicit bias**

- Unconscious attitudes or stereotypes that shape decisions. They often clash with your stated beliefs.
- Having bias doesn't make you racist or sexist. It is a normal brain shortcut.
- System 1 is fast, automatic and associative. System 2 is slow and deliberate. System 1 steers most behaviour.
- Bias has its strongest effect under fatigue, stress, hunger, time pressure and heavy cognitive load.
- Explicit attitudes have improved over decades. Implicit attitudes have not.
- It drives confirmation bias and self-fulfilling prophecies. Expecting mistrust makes you act guarded, which creates mistrust.

**Implicit Association Test (IAT)**

- A timed sorting task that is hard to fake.
- 75 to 80% of white Americans, providers included, show implicit anti-Black bias.
- It predicts behaviour better than self-report. It has been criticized, so treat results as a pointer to blind spots, not a verdict.

**Bias in care (CPSO examples)**

- Women are 3 times less likely to be referred for knee replacement when it is indicated.
- Black and Hispanic patients get less pain medication, at lower doses, despite higher pain scores.
- Suicidal ideation is undertreated in older patients, though men aged 85 to 89 are at high risk.
- COPD is underdiagnosed in women.
- Lesbian and bisexual women get fewer timely Pap tests.

**Effect on the visit**

- More bias means less patient-centred care: shorter visits, less warmth, faster speech, more verbal dominance.
- Patients sense it. Trust drops, and so do adherence and follow-up.
- The physician often thinks the visit went well.
- Differential attribution: an out-group patient gets called "non-adherent", while an in-group patient "forgot the timing".

**Reducing bias: van Ryn's 3 strategies**

1. **Perspective-taking.** Picture the visit from the patient's side, then check it with them: "If I were in your shoes I might feel X. Am I close?"
2. **Partnership-building.** Use "we" and "let's", and name shared goals. This creates a common in-group.
3. **Protecting cognitive resources.** Sleep, eat and manage stress, and fix system pressures like scheduling and staffing.

Also from the readings:

- Sukhera: normalize bias, practise self-compassion, use humility.
- Capatosto: seek counter-stereotypic contact with the groups you hold bias toward.

**Social determinants of health**

- The social and economic conditions that shape health outcomes, mostly outside the health system.
- In the case (Andrea, a PSW and single mother): income, employment and working conditions, gender and caregiving, access to health services, social supports, and health behaviours such as smoking.

**Income sources in Ontario**

- Employment, with or without employer benefits
- Employment Insurance (EI): regular benefits, or sickness benefits
- WSIB for work injuries: covers wages, drugs and physio
- Ontario Works (OW): basic financial assistance
- Ontario Disability Support Program (ODSP): disability income support
- CPP retirement, CPP Disability, and OAS and GIS for seniors
- Private disability insurance

**Drug and physio coverage** *(outside the module, from Ontario government pages, checked Nov 4)*

- **Ontario Drug Benefit (ODB):** covers people 65 and over, people under 25 without a private plan (OHIP+), OW and ODSP recipients, long-term care residents, home care clients, and Trillium enrollees.
- **Trillium Drug Program:** for people with high drug costs relative to income. It has an income-based deductible.
- **OHIP-funded physio at community clinics:** for age 19 and under, 65 and over, after a hospital stay or day surgery in the last 12 months, or on OW or ODSP. No referral needed.

**Making the plan fit the patient**

- Ask about drug or extended health coverage, and whether they can afford a prescription or physio.
- Ask whether the problem started at work. If it did, it opens a WSIB claim.
- Ask about time off, transportation and childcare.
- Check the plan against the patient's reality before they leave.

**Underrepresented populations**

- **Newcomer with limited English:** use a professional interpreter, not family. Speak plainly and use teach-back. Refugee claimants are covered by the Interim Federal Health Program (IFHP). Ask about cultural views of pain and treatment.
- **History of opioid use disorder:** avoid diagnostic overshadowing and take the pain seriously. Use non-opioid multimodal care first. Check the Narcotics Monitoring System before prescribing. Offer opioid agonist therapy and naloxone, and use non-stigmatizing language.
- **Schizophrenia or bipolar disorder:** avoid diagnostic overshadowing and do the full workup. Check interactions, since NSAIDs raise lithium levels. Ask about ODSP, which covers drugs and physio. Keep the plan simple, and involve their case worker with consent.
- **Older adult with mild cognitive impairment:** presume capacity and speak to them directly. Get their consent before involving family. Avoid oral NSAIDs and use acetaminophen or a topical NSAID. Screen for falls risk.

## Week 12: Environmental impact on health (lymphadenopathy)

The syllabus links this week to the MCC objective Health and the Climate Crisis/Environment.

**Module objectives**

- Describe how environmental and climate factors contribute to or trigger illness.
- Identify patient groups vulnerable to environmental health risks.
- Prescribe lifestyle choices that improve both patient and environmental health.

**Required readings**

- McCally: environment and health overview (CMAJ 2000)
- Galanis and Ogden: climate change and emerging diseases (CMAJ)
- Chekuri and Harper: climate-smart health care and the family physician (AFP)
- Clapp: environment and cancer (CMAJ 2000)

**The case: Sarah, 21**

- Fever and right groin swelling for 1 week.
- T 38.6, HR 115, BP 96/60.
- Multiple tender, soft, rubbery 1 to 2 cm groin nodes. No cervical or axillary nodes. Possible mild splenomegaly.
- A 5 to 6 cm circular red rash on the back of her upper leg.
- Camped at Pinery Provincial Park 2 weeks ago.
- Worked in a chemistry lab over the summer.
- Father had colon cancer in his early 50s. Mother has lupus.

**Reading the case** *(outside the module, from the case write-up)*

- A circular rash after camping, with fever, points to Lyme disease. The rash is erythema migrans.
- Pinery is a known tick area in Ontario, and warmer temperatures expand tick range. That is the climate link.
- Lyme disease is a disease of public health significance, so it is reportable to public health.
- Differential for fever with lymphadenopathy: mono, cat scratch disease, STIs (syphilis, HSV, LGV), lymphoma, local skin infection.
- The lab solvents are likely a distractor, but the module asks you to discuss solvents, UV and pesticides.

**Discussion themes**

- Part A: differential for fever with lymphadenopathy, investigations, management, and what "disease of public health significance" means.
- Part B: how heat and temperature shifts drive vector-borne disease, and how UV, herbicides, pesticides and solvents affect health. Also personal climate resilience, cutting the footprint of hospitals and clinics, and advocacy.
