## Lymph Nodes

- Lymph nodes contain lymphocytes to filter lymph fluid to identify and fight infections
- Peak mass of lymphoid tissue occurs in adolescence
- In adults, lymph nodes are not normally palpable except in the inguinal region
- In primary care setting >98% enlarged nodes are non-malignant

- Primary lymphoid tissues = bone marrow (B-cells), thymus (T-cells) à facilitate differentiation of antigen-specific lymphocytes
- Secondary lymphoid tissues = spleen, lymph nodes, GALT/MALT à facilitate the initiation of an immune response
- Mature, but naïve, B- and T-cells leave the primary lymphoid tissues and re-circulate through the body looking for antigens
- T-cells require an intermediate APC for activation (TCR – MHC)
- Immature DCs mature through innate recognition of pathogens; relocate to T-cell zone, expresses CD80/86 (B7.1/B7.2)
- 3-signal hypothesis: antigen-specific activation signal (TCR), co-stimulatory signal (CD-28 ↔ CD 80/86), cytokine signalling
- B-cells become plasma cells; activation can be T-cell independent (low-affinity IgM) or T-cell dependent (high-affinity)
- Germinal center generates high-affinity BCRs – proliferation and somatic hypermutation of the BCR variable region

## Lymphadenopathy

- Lymphadenopathy: abnormality in the size or character of lymph nodes
- Can be isolated (single node), localized (1 anatomic area) or generalized (> 1 anatomic area)
- Majority in primary care setting have non-specific causes
- Common in children (41% in 2-5 years, 90% in 4-8 years) – benign causes more likely at younger age

## Clinical Features Concerning for Malignancy

- Systemic symptoms – fever > 1 week, fatigue, night sweats, weight loss (>10% of body weight)
- Supraclavicular nodes
- Generalized lymphadenopathy – malignant or other systemic condition
- Fixed non-tender nodes, matted nodes, in the absence of other symptoms
- Non-tender nodes > 1 cm with onset in the neonatal period
- Non-tender nodes ≥ 2 cm increasing from baseline or non-resolving after 2 weeks of antibiotics
- Abnormal CXR – mediastinal mass, hilar adenopathy
- Abnormal CBC – lymphoblasts, cytopenias
- Absence of ear, nose, and throat symptoms
- Persistently elevated or rising ESR or CRP (inflammatory markers)

## Multiple Myeloma

- Plasma cells: mature B lymphocytes whose primary function is immunoglobulin/antibody production – adaptive response
  - Differentiate and grow in the BM; require blood supply and contact with BM stroma
  - Do not express CD20
  - Each produce one type of Ig – inner layer = heavy chain (M, A, D, G, E); outer layer = light chains (kappa, lambda)
Multiple Myeloma = plasma cell cancer; production and accumulation of non-functional monoclonal antibodies (M protein)
Progression · Plasmacytomas = solid myeloma tumours; solitary = good prognosis, multiple = end stage disease
Amyloid light-chain (AL) amyloidosis: a type of amyloidosis that develops due to changes in M-proteins that make them insoluble, leading them
to deposit in organs and tissues (heart, kidneys) – may be idiopathic, primary, or secondary to myeloma
POEMS syndrome = polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, M-protein, skin changes – may be primary, or secondary to myeloma

- Repair of sublethal damage to cells between fractions caused by radiation
- Repopulation or regrowth of cells between fractions
- Redistribution of cells into radiosensitive phases of cell cycle
- Reoxygenation of hypoxic cells to make them more sensitive to radiation

## Critical Appraisal

- EBM = patient values + clinical expertise + best research evidence
- Descriptive research: correlational, qualitative study interview, case studies/reports
- Analytical research: observational (cohort, case-control, cross-sectional), experimental (RCT, non-RCT, community trial)
- Equipoise: an ethical concept in the design and conduct of clinical trials
1. There is no evidence to suggest that any treatment or therapy in the trial is better than another
2. There is no evidence to suggest that there is any therapy external to the trial that is better than the treatments being
evaluated in the trial
- Chance = random error; bias = systematic error
- Methods to minimize bias: RCTs, double blind (masked), analysis (intention to treat)
- Confounding: an association between two variables of interest (exposure and an outcome) that appears to be direct and causal,
but in fact exists because of an unconsidered variable(s) and thus leads to a spurious interpretation of their relationship
1. Must be an extraneous risk factor for the disease – related to the outcome
2. Must be associated with the exposure under study in the population
3. Must not be in the casual pathway between the exposure and disease
- To have a fair and unbiased evaluation of randomized treatments, the outcome measures should ideally be evaluated in a
blinded manner, where both the patient and the investigator responsible for evaluation of outcome are unaware of the
treatment assignment (“double blinded”)
  - Open label: all parties are aware of the treatment allocation
  - Single blind: either the patient or the clinician is unaware of the treatment allocation
  - Double blind: both the patient and the investigators are unaware of the treatment allocation
  - Triple blind: the patient, investigator, and analysts are unaware of the treatment allocation
  - Unblinding: the disclosure of the allocation of treatment
- Components of a study protocol: background and rationale for the study, study objective, overview of study design and
graphical schema, patient eligibility and selection criteria, patient enrolment and randomization, treatment regimens, endpoints
(primary, secondary), clinical evaluations, statistical considerations (sample size calculation, power statement, analysis plan),
data collection methods, data safety and monitoring, patient plain language statement and informed consent form

- Superiority trial: a trial with the primary objective of showing that the response to the investigational product is superior to a
comparative agent
  - A new therapy is compared to placebo control, or a new therapy is expected to be better than an existing therapy
- Non-inferiority trial: a trial with the primary objective of showing that the response to the investigational product is not
clinically inferior to a comparative agent
  - A new experimental therapy might not be more efficacious than existing proven therapy, but it may offer other
advantages – safety (less invasive/toxic), tolerability, convenience of administration/cost savings
- Equivalence trial: a trial with the primary objective of showing that the response to two or more treatments differs by an
amount which is clinically unimportant
  - This is usually demonstrated by showing that the true treatment difference is likely to lie between a lower and an
upper equivalence margin of clinically acceptable differences
- Intention-to-treat analysis: analysis of study groups according to which treatment they were assigned, and not according to
which treatment they actually received in the end
  - Everyone randomized in the trial is analyzed and all the follow-up information is included in the analysis, even if they
discontinued the assigned treatment
  - Considered as a de-facto standard for analysis and most journals and regulatory authorities insist on reporting this
  - Almost always yields a more conservative estimate of the treatment effect and reflects real world setting
- Per-protocol (PP) analysis: includes all patients who completed the full course of assigned treatment
  - Will usually yield a higher effect estimate of the treatment effect in this setting
  - In general, the patients who did not comply with therapy and therefore omitted from a per-protocol analysis are those
who are unable to tolerate the treatment or are doing poorly
- Non-inferiority margin/delta: the maximum clinically acceptable difference in efficacy between the new and existing therapy
that one is willing to give up
  - Must be pre-specified
  - Is based on clinical judgement and statistical reasoning
  - Is how much the control arm efficacy can exceed the new treatment arm efficacy such that the new treatment can still
be considered non-inferior to the control therapy
  - A positive non-inferiority clinical trial implies that the experimental therapy is better than or no worse than the control
therapy by at least the value of the delta/non-inferiority margin
- Primary endpoint: the main outcome measure that forms the basis for determination of the power of the study or the sample
size required; commonly dichotomous (success or failure; null hypothesis vs. alternative hypothesis)
- Composite endpoints: consist of 2 or more binary endpoints and may be the primary endpoint of a clinical trial
  - Patients experiencing any of the events from the eligible set (the components) of endpoints are considered to have
experienced the composite endpoint of interest
  - All individual endpoints within composite endpoint are equally weighted
  - To avoid the problem of introducing bias from competing risks, it is important that the composite endpoint consists of
the events of interest and “anything worse” such as death
- p-value – usually &lt; 0.05; consider the total study population and the number of events in each group
- Confidence interval – usually 95%
- One-sided test: investigators suspect a difference in response between two groups in one direction only; smaller sample size
- Two-sided test: investigators are unsure of the direction of difference in response between two groups
- Type I error (𝜶)= false positive rate; Type II error (𝜷) = false negative rate
- Power: the probability of finding a difference between groups when a true treatment difference exists; power = 1 – 𝜷
- Subgroup analysis: performed to examine whether treatment differences appear consistent across all types of patients or vary
according to subgroups

The Immune System (for Dummies like Nicole… or if you really want to understand it)
There are four major components of the immune system involved in fighting off pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites):
1. Antibodies and the cells that make them
2. Complement
3. T cells
4. Non-specific effector cells

## Innate Immunity

- Clotting factors activate the cleavage of complement molecules into reactive subunits that have effects on local innate cells and
the vasculature (initially restricted)
- Local mast cells and tissue macrophages can recognize certain types of molecules by their molecular pattern
  - Pattern recognition receptors (PRR) – toll-like receptors (TLR)
  - Pathogen associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) – recognize bacterial lipopolysaccharide, CpG motifs
  - Damage associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) – recognize molecules released in response to tissue damage
- The activation of mast cells causes the secretion of histamine, resulting in increased permeability (tumor, dolor) and
vasodilation (rubor, calor)
  - Blood flows into the area of the injury
  - Plasma containing antibodies and compliment flows out of the blood and into the tissue
- The activation of macrophages leads to the secretion of cytokines and chemokines and attraction of other leukocytes to the site
  - Cytokines influence the expression of adhesion molecules on the surface of vascular endothelial cells
    - Important cytokines: TNF, IL-1
    - Important chemokines: IL-8, C3a
  - Neutrophils, T cells, and more macrophages come flooding into the site of inflammation
- Local dendritic cells that have been stimulated to be phagocytic by the inflammatory response take up the digested bacterial
material and travel to the local draining lymph node, where they present antigen to T cells and provide antigen for B cell uptake

## Antibodies and the Cells that Make Them

- Antibodies/immunoglobulins recognize specific antigen and bind to pathogens to bring them to the attention of other parts of
the immune system (complement, phagocytic cells)
- Antibody is made up of four polypeptide chains held together by disulphide bonds
  - 2 identical heavy chains – μ, δ, γ, α, ε
  - 2 identical light chains – kappa, lambda
- Fab region = N-terminal ends of the light chain + N-terminal ends of the heavy
chain à antigen binding region
  - Consists of a hypervariable area that is encoded by gene elements that
undergo random gene rearrangement
  - 5’ to 3’: 300-1000 variable heavy (VH), 13 diversity heavy (DH; heavy only),
4 joining heavy (JH), constant (C) regions
  - During development of young B cells, they undergo immunoglobulin
gene rearrangement, where splicing, deletion, and recombination occurs
to make one gene sequence
  - Great structural diversity to recognize any and every antigen the body encounters
- Fc region = C-terminal ends of the heavy chain à biological activity of the antibody
  - Binds to receptors on phagocytic cells to induce phagocytosis
  - Binds to Fc receptors on mast cells before the antibody reacts with antigen
  - Binds to complement to initiate the complement activation pathway

## Pre-B cell to B cell

- For a B cell to be functional, it needs to have a functional antibody on its surface
- B cells develop from stem cells in the bone marrow
- Pre-B cells rearrange the gene for antibody heavy chains, attach to surrogate light chains, and are brought to the cell surface
- Heavy chain passes the test if the composite molecule on the surface activates Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) – allelic exclusion
- Failure to activate BTK will lead to pre-B cell death – gets a second chance with the second allele
- Successful light chain gene rearrangement leads to the expression of fully developed IgM and IgD on the surface of the B cell,
which marks the transition from pre-B cell to a mature naïve B cell

## Naïve B cell = has not seen an antigen yet

- New naïve B cells enter the circulation and travel through the blood to lymphoid organs
- Each naïve B cell has a different antibody on its surface; each individual B cell has hundreds of thousands of antibodies on its
surface that are identical and all bind to the same site on the recognized antigen
- Once a B cell makes surface IgM, it can then make a different class of antibody (class switch), which involves a change in the Fc
region of the antibody, NOT in the Fab region – changes the class and function of the antibody, but not its antigen specificity
- In the lymph nodes, naïve B cells may encounter an antigen recognized by their surface antibodies
- Many (more than 99%) naïve B cells circulate their entire life span without encountering antigen, and die within a few days

## B cell activation

- B cells can recognize antigen in its native form as soluble protein, unlike T cells which require antigen to be degraded and
presented on the surface of an antigen presenting cell in the context of MHC
- If the B cell comes in contact with the antigen, the B cell becomes activated
- After the antigen binds to the surface antibody on the B cells, receptor-mediated endocytosis takes place
- The antibody and antigen are internalized by the B cell in an endocytic vesicle
- Digestive enzymes break the antigen down into individual peptides
- Free peptides bind to MHC in the open grooves of the MHC II molecule on the vesicle membrane

## T and B cell interaction

1. B cells present antigen plus MHC II to an activated CD4+ T helper (Th) cell specific for that antigen
2. The B and T cells form a conjugate, and cytokines are produced and released by T cells
3. Cytokine induced signals (IL-4) in the B cell stimulate proliferation and differentiation

## B cell differentiation into plasma cells

- B cells are small lymphocytes and have a thin rim of cytoplasm around the nucleus
- Plasma cells are much larger and contain abundant endoplasmic reticulum and cytoplasm – produce and secrete antibody
- Many B cells differentiate into plasma cells, a few differentiate into B memory cells, and some daughter cells continue to divide

## Antibody secretion

- Plasma cells are the final stage of development of B cells which have recognized antigen, been stimulated by helper T cell-
derived cytokines, and secrete the antibodies found in the circulation
- Primary response (IgM, some IgG): the first time antigen is seen – blood level of antibodies takes a few days to increase
- Secondary response (IgG): persistence of the antigen or re-exposure to the same antigen is much faster and more robust

## Memory B cells

- The secondary response is dependent on long-lived memory B cells
- Generated in lymphoid tissue after B cell activation and proliferation; reside in BM, lymph nodes, mucosal sites, and spleen
- Express high affinity surface antibodies which enable them to be activated by lower levels of antigen than naïve B cells

## Antigens and antigen-antibody interaction

- Antigen: a substance capable of inducing a specific immune response – generate an antibody response
- Usually foreign proteins that enter the body via an infection, but sometimes the body’s own proteins are treated like antigens
- The pathogen itself is not an antigen – the proteins and carbohydrates that make up the protein are antigens
- A pathogen could have thousands of molecules that would be recognized as antigens by the immune system – epitopes
- Antigens are recognized by the immune system by B cells (surface antibody) and T cells (T cell receptor)
- Antibodies can recognize antigen on the surface of bacteria or viruses, as well as antigen free-floating in the tissues

## Complement

- The complement cascade consists of a series of protein proenzymes present in the blood that are converted to active enzymes
by interaction with pathogens and with each other
  - It resembles the coagulation pathway, and coagulation often results in the complement cascade being activated
Two major ways of activating the complement cascade:
1. Classical pathway: C1, C4, and C2 are activated by the Fc regions of IgG or IgM after the antibody has bound to antigen
2. Alternate pathway: Factor B, D, and P (properdin) are directly activated by certain pathogens
- The classical and alternate pathways converge on C3 (split into C3a and C3b)
  - C3a = activates mast cells to release mediators which cause local vascular permeability and vasodilation; chemotactic
factor for phagocytic immune cells
  - C3b = binds directly to the surface of foreign organisms/immune complexes and tags them for destruction by
phagocytes; reacts directly with other components of the complement cascade (C5-9) to produce the MAC attack
There are four major outcomes of activating this cascade:
1. Local vasodilation and increased vascular permeability – C3a, C4a, C5a = anaphylatoxins
    - Binding of anaphylatoxins to their receptors on mast cells and basophils induces the cell to degranulate,
releasing mediators like histamine
    - Smooth muscle dilation on the arteriolar side in the vasculature à more blood flow to local capillaries
    - Increased permeability of endothelial lining of the blood vessels à more fluid, IgG, and complement
2. Attraction of immune cells, especially phagocytes – C3a, C5a, chemokines
    - Chemotaxis: the process whereby an immune cell is attracted to, and moves toward, a soluble factor
    - Chemokines: soluble protein molecules that have the effect of attracting cells to a particular site
    - Phagocytes are recruited to the tissue to ingest the invading organism, antigens, or debris from inflammation
    - Immune cells do NOT leak out of the blood because of increased vascular permeability like plasma proteins
and water do – they must be attracted to the site by chemotaxis and penetrate through the endothelial lining
of the vascular (transmigration)
3. Opsonization of foreign organisms for phagocytosis – IgG, C3b
    - Opsonization: the process where particles/microorganisms become coated/tagged with molecules which bind
to specific receptors on phagocytes
    - C3b non-specifically binds to foreign organisms/immune complexes during complement activation
    - Phagocytes have surface receptors for C3b (CR3) – they bind, ingest, and degrade the tagged microorganisms
4. Destruction of invading organisms by the membrane attack complex (MAC attack) – C5b, C6, C7, C8, C9
    - C5 is broken down into C5a and C5b after binding to C5 convertase
    - C5a diffuses away and has anaphylatoxin and chemotactic activity
    - C5b binds directly to the target organism and becomes the binding site for other complement components
    - C5b will quickly degrade unless c6 binds to it, creating C5b6, to which C7 will then bind
    - C7 changes the conformation of the proteins so that they are able to insert into lipid membranes
    - C8 and C9 bind to the complex, creating the MAC
    - C5b678 will bind up to 16 molecules of C9
    - MAC looks like a tube with a pore in the center and is hydrophobic (like a straw)
    - MAC inserts into the membrane of the organism or cell and allows ion, water, and other small molecules to
freely pass through the pore; the organism will not be able to maintain osmotic integrity and will quickly die

## T Cells

- T cells have antigen recognition molecules (T cell receptor – TcR) on the surface which are composed of α and β chains
1. The α and β chains are associated with a group of 5 linked proteins, collectively referred to as CD3
- Like antibody, the TcR has both a variable region (created by gene rearrangement) and a constant region
- T cell receptor complex = α chain + β chain + associated CD3 proteins
- The TcR will NOT bind to free antigen

## T cell development

- For a T cell to be functional, it needs to have a functional T cell receptor on its surface that is able to recognize antigen as a
peptide in the groove of self MHC, and is appropriately linked to CD3 for proper signal transduction
- Pre-T cells arise from lymphoid stem cell precursors in the bone marrow, and travel in the bloodstream to the thymus
1. The α chain of the TcR undergoes gene rearrangement; α chain is brought to the surface with a surrogate α chain and tested
1. If it links properly with the signal transduction components, then the T cell goes on to rearrange β chain genes and put
a mature T cell receptor on its surface – at this stage, the T cell is both CD4+ and CD8+ (double positive cell)
2. The next step is to make sure the T cell can recognize self MHC – T cells interact with MHC bearing cells in the thymus
2. If the TcR binds weakly to the MHC bearing cells, then the cell goes on to further development
3. If the TcR does not bind to the MHC bearing cells, then it does not receive life prolonging signals and dies (most cells)
4. If the TcR binds very strongly to the MHC bearing cells, indicating that the self-peptide and the self-MHC is their chosen
antigen to respond to, then they will be killed
- If the T cell passes both of these tests, it will become a mature double positive T cell; it will then lose either CD4 or CD8 to
become a naïve CD4+ cell (helper T cell) or a naïve CD8+ cell (cytotoxic T cell)

## Helper T cell (Th) activation

- Th cell activation is initiated by the interaction of TcR-CD3 complex with antigen-MHC II molecules on the surface of an APC
- This interaction initiates a cascade of biochemical events in the T cell that results in growth and proliferation of the T cell
  - Occurs primarily through an increase in IL-2 secretion by the T cell and an increase in IL-2 receptors on the T cell surface
  - IL-2 is a potent T cell growth cytokine which acts in an autocrine (self-stimulating) fashion to promote the growth,
clonal proliferation, and differentiation of the T cell recently stimulated by antigen
  - All of its progeny will recognize the same T cell epitope of the original antigen, since they all have identical TcR
  - Naïve bystander T cells have no IL-2 receptors and thus cannot be involved
- The activation is aided by the CD4 molecule on Th cells, as well as accessory molecules (CD45, CD28, CD2)
- The APC also provides a co-stimulatory/second signal through B7 molecules (CD28:CD80/86) and cytokines (IL-1, IL-12)
- Activated Th cells continue to become effector cells and help B cells become antibody producing plasma cells
  - Th1: induced by IL-12, IFN-𝛄; secretes IL-2, IFN-𝛄, TNF-𝛃 à autoimmune responses, cell-mediated immunity
  - Th2: induced by IL-4; secretes IL-4, IL-5, IL-6 à promotes IgE/eosinophil responses, humoral immunity

## Antigen presenting cells

- Antigen presenting cells (APC): a functionally defined group of cells which are able to take up antigens and present them to T
lymphocytes in a recognizable form (in the groove of an MHC II molecule)
- APC internalizes the antigen, processes it (breaks it down into antigenic peptides with lysozymes), and then expresses the
antigen peptide on its surface
- Dendritic cells are the most efficient and highly effective – they leave the site of infection and travel to the lymph node
- Macrophages and B cells can present antigen to activated T cells and memory T cells

## Cytotoxic T cell (Tc) activation

- The TcR on the CD8+ cell must interact with processed antigen on MHC I on the surface of an APC
- The CD8+ must be stimulated by cytokines – IL-2 is supplied by activated Th cells in the vicinity or is self-produced by CD8+ cells
- Like resting Th cells, resting Tc do not express IL-2 receptors; antigen stimulation increases the expression of IL-2 receptors on
the cell surface, so only the cells recognizing the antigen will become activated and proliferate
- Activated Tc cells mature into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL), which are involved in the recognition and direct killing of
pathogen infected self-cells (not pathogens themselves)
1. Conjugate formation between the CTP and the target cell
2. Membrane attack on the target cell
    - Cytokines, enzymes, and perforin are released from the granules of the CTL by exocytosis
3. Target cell death
    - Granzyme is transported into the target cell and activates a cell suicide pathway which involves caspases
    - Apoptosis is induced
    - Fas ligand (FasL) is also expressed by CTL and binds to Fas on the target cell to activate the caspase pathway

## Effector Cells of the Immune System

- Monocytes circulate in the blood after leaving the BM before they enter the tissue to mature into macrophages
- Macrophages are activated in response to Th-derived cytokines (IFN) or from contact with bacteria or bacterial products
1. Phagocytosis – recognize and remove unwanted particulate matter (products of inflammation, invading organisms,
immune complexes, toxins, dying cells)
2. Antigen presenting cells during secondary immune responses – activate memory T cells; quicker than transporting the
antigen to the local draining lymph node for presentation to T cells
3. Cytokine secretion – IL1, IL6, TNF
- Natural killer (NK) cells = large granular lymphocytes (LGL)
  - Have some surface markers in common with T cells, and are functionally similar to CTL (but do not have a TcR)
  - Unlike CTL, NK cells do not need to recognize specific antigen presented by MCH on the target cell to kill it
  - Produce large amounts of TNF-α à binds to TNF receptor on target cells and induced apoptosis
- Neutrophils = polymorphonuclear cells (PMN)
  - Very short-lived cells (8 hours after release from BM)
  - Attracted into the tissue by chemotactic factors – first cells to arrive at the site of inflammation
  - Active phagocytic cells in the tissues – oxygen dependent pathways (O2–, H2O2), nitrogen dependent pathways (NO),
independent pathways (defensins, digestive enzymes)
- Eosinophils – phagocytic cells
- Mast cells (in the tissues) – high affinity Fc receptors for IgE; stimulated by anaphylatoxins and crosslinking of surface IgE
  - Contain numerous granules with preformed mediators which can be released after stimulation (histamine, TNF-α)
  - Stimulation also results in the production of prostaglandins and leukotrienes
- Basophils (in the blood) – high affinity Fc receptors for IgE; involved in Type I hypersensitivity responses

## Cytokines

- Cytokines: protein messenger molecules produced by immune system cells in order to communicate and orchestrate the attack
  - Maintain the normal growth, migration, and survival of immune cells
  - Includes chemokines, interleukins, and growth factor subfamilies
- The same cytokine may be made by a number of different cells
- The same cytokine may have different effects in different circumstances (pleiotropy)
- Different cytokines may have the same activity depending on the situation (redundancy)
- Cytokines often act together and increase the effects of one another (synergy)
- Most cytokines have paracrine or autocrine effects
