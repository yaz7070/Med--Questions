## Microbiology Lab

- A laboratory devoted to the culturing, examination, and identification of microorganisms
- Team: medical microbiologist, manager, coordinator, laboratory technologists, laboratory assistance
- Sections: bacteriology, virology, mycology, parasitology, serology, molecular
- Role: diagnosis and treatment of infectious diseases, infection control, public health

## Laboratory Test Information Guide (LTIG)

- Test purpose and limitations
- Patient selection criteria
- Timing of specimen collection
- Optimal specimen collection sites
- Approved specimen collection methods
- Specimen transport medium criteria/transport time/temperature
- Specimen holding instructions if it cannot be transported immediately
- Minimum acceptable volume
- Availability of test
- Turnaround time
- Result reporting procedures

## Rejection Criteria

- Unlabeled/mislabeled/incompletely labeled specimens
- Quantity not sufficient for testing
- Use of an improper transport medium
- Use of improper swab
- Specimen inappropriately handled (temperature, timing, storage)
- Improper collection site for test requested
- Specimen leakage from transport container

## Blood Culture

- Volume of blood collected is most critical
- Disinfect the venipuncture site
- Catheter-drawn blood cultures have a higher risk of contamination
- Do not submit catheter tips for culture without an accompanying blood culture obtained by venipuncture

## CNS Infection

- Meningitis, encephalitis, focal infections of brain parenchyma, CNS shunt infections, subdural empyema, epidural abscess
- Routes of infection: hematogenous spread, direct spread from an infected site, anatomic defects in CNS structures, nerves
- Ix: culture, molecular (viral – enterovirus, herpesvirus), Ag detection (fungal – cryptococcus)
- Blood cultures should be obtained if meningitis is suspected
- Inform the microbiology laboratory if unusual organisms are suspected
- Collect 3-4 tubes of CSF by lumbar puncture and submit for bacterial culture and molecular testing
- Collect as much sample as possible

## Antimicrobial Susceptibility

- Susceptibility testing: used to produce antimicrobial susceptibility profiles and detect resistance to therapeutic agents
- Standardization: optimizes bacterial growth, optimizes conditions, maintains reproducibility
- Breakpoint: minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) or zone diameter value used to categorize S, SDD, I, R, NS
- Interpretive category: derived from microbiological characteristic, PK-PD parameters, and clinical outcome data

## Clostridial Species

- Gram-positive bacilli, anaerobic, spore forming, +/– toxin-producing
- Spore: a unit of sexual or asexual reproduction that may be adapted for dispersal and for survival, often for extended periods of
time, in unfavourable conditions; metabolically inactive; hard to destroy
- Ix: nucleic acid amplification test (C. difficile toxin gene), ELISA (C. difficile common antigen, C. difficile toxin); difficult to culture

| Clostridial Species | Diseases Caused |
|---|---|
| Clostridium difficile | Diarrhea; pseudomembranous colitis |

Large clostridial toxins TcdA and TcdB: enter epithelial cells to cause inflammatory cytokine (IL1-β) and chemokine production, influx of
neutrophils, disruption of tight junctions, fluid secretion, and cell death (TcdB is most potent)
CDTb binds to epithelial receptor and is endocytosed; CDTb oligomerizes and forms a pore
CDTa is translocated through the pore and enters the cytoplasm as the endosome acidifies (vacuolar ATPase); CDTa ribosylates actin,
leading to complete destruction of the cellular actin cytoskeleton, loss of cell shape and tight junctions, and cell death

Viruses à acute and chronic infections
- Viruses: infectious, obligate intracellular parasites composed of DNA or RNA surrounded by a protein coat and/or membrane
- Virions: mature virus particles that can be released into the extracellular environment
- Capsid: protein shell that encases the genome, held together by non-covalent interactions; capsid + genome = nucleocapsid
- Enveloped viruses: have an outer lipid bilayer, acquired by the budding of nucleocapsid through the host cell’s cytoplasmic
membrane; glycoproteins are expressed on the surface of the envelope
- Naked viruses: have no envelope and are often more resistant to harsh conditions
- Virus morphology: helical, icosahedral (hollow, symmetrical, quasi-spherical), complex (non-symmetrical)
- Virion components: polymerases, proteases, integrases topoisomerases, capping enzymes, proteins, lipids
- The genome is the key unit that defines the virus
  - Gene products and regulatory signals for replication, assembly and packaging, regulation and timing of the replication
cycle, modulation of host defenses, and spread to other cells and host is encoded in the viral genome
- mRNA is always the (+) strand
- DNA of equivalent polarity is also the (+) strand
- Compliments of (+) strands are negative (–) strands
- Virus replication cycle
1. Attachment to cell receptors
2. Entry – penetration into the cytoplasm
3. Uncoating – genome and other contents released from nucleocapsid
4. Protein synthesis – translation of viral mRNA
5. Genome replication – new copies of genome
6. Assembly of new virions
7. Release of new virus from the cell – off to infect a new cell

## Body Temperature

- Thermal compartments: core = internal organs and muscles; shell = skin and subcutaneous fat
  - Fluctuations of temperature in the peripheral regions help maintain the core body temperature
- Normal oral temperature = 37°C (35.5-37.7°C) – no single temperature can be used as the upper limit of normal
- Physiological variations: circadian rhythm (peak temperature = 2 PM; lowest temperature = 4 AM), menstrual cycle, post-
menopausal hot flashes and fever
- The homeostatic control of body temperature (regulated variable) involves a negative feedback system composed of sensors, an
integrating center, and effectors
  - Thermoreceptors: sensor neurons that are activated by warmth or cold – skin, spinal cord, abdominal viscera, brain
  - Hypothalamus integrates the input from thermoreceptors – anterior = heat (front, fire), posterior = cold (back, brrr)
- Feedforward mechanism: the body acts in anticipation of thermal changes
- 1°C increase in body temperature = 4.4 bpm increase in HR
- Thermoregulation reaches adult levels once puberty ends – girls = 13-14 years; boys = 17-18 years

## Fever

- Fever: a state of elevated core body temperature that is often, but not necessarily, part of the host defense against invasion by
microorganisms or inanimate matter recognized as pathogenic or foreign
- Early morning (6 AM) oral/tympanic temperature ≥ 37.2°C (99.0°F); any oral/tympanic temperature ≥ 37.8°C (100°F)
- Fever inhibits the growth of many microorganisms, increases the production of antibodies, and stimulates the innate and
adaptive immune responses to infections
- Common symptoms: rise in body temperature (with subsequent sweating and falling of body temperature), chills, rigors
- Variable symptoms: headache, malaise, anorexia
- Fever of unknown origin: temperature ≥ 101°F documented clinically on several separate occasions, where an appropriate
initial diagnostic workup does not reveal etiology of fever
