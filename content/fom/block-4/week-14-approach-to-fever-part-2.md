## Transmission of Microorganisms and Chain of Infection

- Not all exposures lead to transmission and infection
- The probability of transmission and infection is dependent on host susceptibility, microorganism inoculum size, viability and
virulence, and the effectiveness of control measures
- Herd immunity: when a high enough proportion of individuals in a population are resistant to an agent, then those few who are
susceptible will be protected by the resistant majority, since the pathogen will be unlikely to “find” the susceptible individuals
- Aerosol-generating medical procedures: intubation, manual ventilation, open endotracheal suctioning, cardiopulmonary
resuscitation, bronchoscopy, sputum induction nebulized therapy, autopsy, non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (CPAP)

## Choosing an Antimicrobial Agent

- Must know the identity (or likely identity) of the infecting organism on the basis of clinical information
- Information about the susceptibility (or likely susceptibility) of the infecting organisms must be as accurate as possible
- Host factors: drug allergies, site of infection, pregnancy, regional susceptibility information, renal/hepatic function (eGFR),
recent antibiotic exposure, community vs. hospital infection, DDIs; comorbidities, immunocompromised, older age
- Drug factors: safety record, success record, microbial etiology, bactericidal/bacteriostatic, penetration into site, adverse effects
- Direct adverse effects: neutropenia/thrombocytopenia/nephrotoxicity/ototoxicity/neurotoxicity, DDIs, allergic reactions
- Indirect adverse effects: effects on normal bowel flora, increased risk of infection with drug-resistant pathogens

## Epidemiologic Terms

- Incubation period: the time from exposure to the causative agent until the first symptoms develop (unique for each disease)
- Infectious period/period of communicability: the time during which an infectious agent may be transferred directly or
indirectly from an infected person to another; may start/stop before, during, or after symptom onset/resolution
- Generation time: the time duration from the onset of infectiousness in a primary case to the onset of infectiousness in a
secondary case infected by the primary case
- Basic reproductive number (R0): the average number of secondary infections produced by a typical case of an infection in a
population where everyone is susceptible
  - R0 > 1 (outbreak expected to continue; exponential growth); R0 &lt; 1 (outbreak expected to end)
- Effective reproductive number I: the average number of secondary cases per infectious case in a population made up of both
susceptible and non-susceptible hosts
  - R > 1 (number of cases will increase); R = 1 (disease is endemic); R &lt; 1 (decline in the number of cases)
- Antigenic drift: slow point mutations over time occurring in one virus; may result in epidemic
- Antigenic shift: genetic elements in two viruses reassort to make a new virus/strain; may result in pandemic

## Vaccine Hesitancy

- Vaccine hesitancy: refers to delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccines despite availability of vaccine services
- Most vaccine-hesitant parents under-immunize their children; 3% of parents are vaccine refusers
1. Pre-visit prep: collection and review of background information – immunization Hx, comorbidities, immunosuppression,
willingness to attend, identified barriers, outright trust issues, language barriers
2. Organize and obtain required supports – cultural competence, interpreter, handouts, statistics (rates, severity, safety, efficacy)

| COVID-19 Vaccine Hesitancy Factor | Reasoning |

## Level of Disease

- Sporadic: a disease that occurs infrequently and irregularly – SARS-CoV-1
- Endemic: the constant presence of a disease in a population, and/or the typical prevalence of that disease – salmonella
- Epidemic: increases in cases of disease above what is expected in a population – influenza in the fall and winter
  - Outbreak is synonymous but often used for a more limited geographic area
- Pandemic: an epidemic that has spread over several countries or continents, usually affecting many people – SARS-CoV-2

## Healthcare-Associated Infections (HAI)

- On any given day, 1 in 31 hospital patients has at least one HAI
- 35-55% of HAI are preventable – highest for central line associated bloodstream infections (CLABI) and catheter associated UTIs
(CAUTI); lower for ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and surgical site infections (SSI)
- Each year, 8000 Canadians die from hospital-acquired infections; 220000 others get infected
- Treatment is more costly than prevention (\$129 million/year) – prolonged hospitalization, special control measures, expensive
treatments (stronger antibiotics), extensive surveillance (MRSA)

## Sepsis

- Sepsis: a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection
- Can be caused by bacteria, viruses, or fungi
- Sepsis is common, it is deadly, and it is an emergency – high morbidity and mortality rate (30-40%)
- Loss of balance of proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory mediators à cellular injury à multiple organ dysfunction (MODS)
- The effects of inflammation are no longer localized – they are systemic
- All organs can be involved in sepsis due to microcirculation dysfunction in the arteriolar capillary networks
  - Cardiovascular (low BP, high HR, myocardial dysfunction), respiratory (low O2, acute respiratory distress syndrome),
brain, coagulation system (thrombocytopenia, DIC), liver, kidneys, muscles, nerves

- Septic shock: characterized by a low blood pressure that does not respond to fluids
  - Blood pressure is low because blood vessels (arterioles) vasodilate, and there is a loss of intravascular fluid into the
interstitial space – add vasoconstrictive agents to artificially increase BP and prevent salt-water drowning/edema
- Therapies that have been studied and do not work: activated protein C/heparin/antithrombin III, tissue factor pathway inhibitor,
nitric oxide synthesis blockade, anti-TNF antibodies/TNF receptor blocking agent, interleukin receptor antibodies, platelet
activating factor (GCSF), anti-endotoxin/LPS inhibition, vitamin C and other antioxidants, immunonutrition, hemofiltration
- It is important to recognize sepsis clinically (low temperature or fevered, tachycardia, high RR, warm/flushed, low BP), and also
try to identify the cause

## Basic Principles of Sepsis Management

Step
Early recognition · Wherever you work, how ever you practice, if you see sick patients, you need a plan to NOT miss sepsis
Physiology: tachypnea/hypoxia from lung dysfunction, tachycardia/hypotension, oliguria from kidney damage
Symptomatology: rapid decline in performance status, family is concerned, major change in cognition/confusion, looks/feels very unwell
High risk factor populations: recent surgery, recent chemotherapy, immunosuppressed, elderly
Early treatment · Nothing saves lives in sepsis like early, appropriate antibiotics – do whatever it takes to get the drug into the patient’s blood ASAP
From the onset of hypotension, minutes count – approximately 8% increase in mortality every hour that they are delayed
Must be broad spectrum, often multidrug therapy
Cultures are important to draw prior to antibiotic, but only if they WILL NOT DELAY ANTIBIOTIC ADMINISTRATION
Source control · Prompt identification of the source – PEx (wound, rash), imaging (CT, ultrasound)
Prompt source control – surgery, drainage, removal of infected foreign body
Life support · Invasive support for failing organs – provides time for the patient to get better (therapy, antibiotics); not fixing the problem
-Cardiovascular: vasopressors, inotropes
-Pulmonary: mechanical ventilation, prone positioning, lung protective ventilation, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)
-Renal: dialysis, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)
-Neurologic: sedation, delirium medication
