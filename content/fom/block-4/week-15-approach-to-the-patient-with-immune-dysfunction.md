## Immunodeficiency

- Immunodeficiency: when the pathways to remove pathogens are disrupted either by germline mutations (primary
immunodeficiency), or secondary to infection, medication, or malignancy
- If history is suggestive of an immunodeficiency, ask more questions to determine what type of immunodeficiency the patient
may have, and do laboratory tests to see if there are any abnormalities that may point to the type of immunodeficiency

## Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)

- Retrovirus that infects CD4 T cells and destroys them, resulting in a progressive loss of cell mediated immunity
- Virus is secreted in secretions (sexual intercourse), blood (sharing needles, blood products), or transplacental transfer
Initial infection:
- HIV is taken up into cells, RNA is transcribed into DNA, gets incorporated into the host genome, viral particles are assembled
- Mild infection symptoms 1 month after initial exposure
- Body will form an immune response specific to HIV infection, viral load will decrease, and symptoms resolve
Clinical latency:
- Virus is still being replicated and destroys CD4 T cells
- Phase lasts 8 years – patients are asymptomatic
- Patients can live a long time symptom free, but do have other long-term health issues associated with HIV infection, such as
cardiovascular disease, diabetes, renal disease, and cancer
- Treatment: cocktail of medications where different cellular mechanisms of viral replication are targeted

## Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS)

- Patients become symptomatic with systemic symptoms and infection seen in primary immunodeficiency with T cell dysfunction
- Weight loss, lymphadenopathy, fever
- Opportunistic infections (Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia), malignancy (Kaposi sarcoma)
- Phase lasts 2 years – results in death

## Anaphylaxis

- Anaphylaxis: a serious allergic reaction with a rapid onset (minutes to within 1 hour), which may cause death
- 0.5-5% lifetime prevalence; 1% of ER visits for acute systemic allergic reactions receive the diagnosis of anaphylaxis
- Caused by the systemic release of mast cell and basophil mediators
- Anaphylaxis is highly likely when any one of the following three criteria is fulfilled:
1. Sudden onset of an illness (minutes to several hours), with involvement of the skin, mucosal tissue, or both
(generalized hives, itch/flush, swollen lips-tongue-uvula), and at least one of the following:
a. Sudden respiratory symptoms and signs (shortness of breath, wheeze, cough, stridor, hypoxemia)
b. Sudden reduced BP or symptoms of end-organ dysfunction (hypotonia/collapse, incontinence)
2. Two or more of the following that occur suddenly after exposure to a likely allergen or other trigger for that patient:
a. Sudden skin or mucosal symptoms and signs (generalized hives, itch/flush, swollen lips-tongue-uvula)
b. Sudden respiratory symptoms and signs (shortness of breath, wheeze, cough, stridor, hypoxemia)
c. Sudden reduced BP or symptoms of end-organ dysfunction (hypotonia/collapse, incontinence)
d. Sudden gastrointestinal symptoms (crampy abdominal pain, vomiting)
3. Reduced BP after exposure to a known allergen for that patient:
a. Infants and children: low systolic BP (age-specific) or greater than 30% decrease in systolic BP
b. Adults: systolic BP of less than 90 mm Hg or greater than 30% decrease from that person’s baseline
- Treatment: remove the trigger, prompt administration of IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg of 1:1000 solution, max dose = 0.3 mg),
repeat epinephrine every 5-15 mins PRN, other drugs after epinephrine (O2, fluids, antihistamine, salbutamol, corticosteroids)
- Mean time to cardiorespiratory arrest: iatrogenic = 5 mins, insect sting = 15 mins, food = 30 mins

## Hygiene Hypothesis (1989)

- Throughout the 20th and into the 21st century, the incidence of allergy has rapidly grown
- Suggests that the lower incidence of early childhood infections could explain the rise in allergy
  - Immune system is being established in infancy and childhood
  - Th1 inflammatory state is allergy protecting – defense against cellular organisms
  - Th2 inflammatory state is allergy promoting – induction of antibody production by B cells
- Living in a clean environment promotes the development of allergy; living in a dirty environment results in allergy protection

## Allergy Testing

- Allergy testing plays a role in the diagnosis of Type I hypersensitivity reactions
- The best allergy testing tool is the clinical Hx
- Allergy testing sensitivity and specificity are different depending on what is being tested for and the type of testing done
- Interpreting a test depends on the pre-/post-test probabilities and likelihood ratios, in the context of the clinical Hx
- Pre-test probability: the clinical impression on the chance that the patient has a certain diagnosis
  - Clinical experience, epidemiology, co-morbid conditions, risk factors, PEx findings
- Likelihood ratio (LR): the likelihood that a given test result would be expected in a patient with the target disorder compared to
the likelihood that that same result would be expected in a patient without the target disorder
  - Positive LR: the odds that a person who has what you are looking for has a positive test compared to a person who
does not have what you are looking for but has a positive test; Sn/(1-Sp)
  - Negative LR: the odds that a person who has what you are looking for has a negative test compared to a person who
does not have what you are looking for but has a negative test; (1-Sn)/Sp
  - Calculate from sensitivity and specificity or use a nomogram
- Cross reactivity: can occur when there is \~70% homology between antigens such that the tertiary structure is conserved
  - Patients with food pollen syndrome to birch can get oral pruritis to almond
- Food allergen proteins used for skin prick testing and serum IgE testing are commercially prepared
  - Purified food protein – should contain all relevant allergens; amount of each allergen is variable with each batch
  - Recombinant extract – known allergens are made using bacteria vectors; often contains the major known allergens,
but will not contain allergens that are not well known (false negative results); can be used to spike purified food
protein extracts for clinically important allergens that are at low concentration
- Environmental allergen proteins: dust mites (feces), cat/dog (dander), seasonal allergens (pollen), mold (spores)

- Radioallergosorbent (RAST) testing: a blood test using radioimmunoassay test to detect specific IgE antibodies in order to

1. Extract proteins from the allergen

4. Add a marker to measure the IgE bound; measure how much IgE is bound
- Component based IgE testing: pinpoints specific proteins that may be causing allergic symptoms
  - Newer test to better predict reactions to foods and potential cross-reactivity to foods, and to identify major allergens

- Oral challenges (gold standard)

    - Dry foods = 8-10 g, wet foods = 100 mL

  - High risk patients: start with very low doses where the patient is unlikely to react, optimize comorbid conditions, avoid
in pregnant patients, beta blockers can interfere with allergic reaction treatments, do the challenge in hospital and
consider an IV prior to the challenge

## Autoimmune Disease

- Immune tolerance: antigen-specific immunological unresponsiveness to self-antigens
  - Central tolerance: immature self-reactive lymphocytes are negatively selected/deleted in the BM and thymus gland
  - Lymphocytes that have low affinity receptors for self-antigen will enter the periphery
  - Peripheral tolerance: mature self-reactive lymphocytes are addressed by the spleen and lymph nodes through
immunologic ignorance (anatomical barrier), anergy/inhibition (blocks CD28:CD80), deletion (Fas ligand), and
suppression (regulatory T cells – IL-10, TGF-β)
- Autoimmunity: failure of the immune system to recognize self-cells, and the resultant destruction of these self-cells

## Granulomas

- Granuloma: an aggregation of macrophages surrounded by T lymphocytes that forms in response to chronic inflammation
- The activated macrophages tend to accumulate cytoplasm and are called epithelioid histiocytes because of their resemblance
to epithelial cells
  - Sometimes their nuclei resemble footprints in the sand
  - Sometimes many macrophages fuse to form multinucleate giant cells (Langhan’s cells)
- Granulomas are often an attempt to contain something that is difficult to eradicate – talc in IV drug use, sutures
- White nodule with a chalky/cheesy center – necrotising/caseating as a result of hypoxia and free radical injury
- Common sites: lungs, lymph nodes, GI tract
- Potential causes: mycobacterial (TB), fungal (histoplasmosis), bacterial (cat scratch disease); sarcoidosis, vasculitis, Chron’s
disease, foreign body reaction, malignancy
- Make sure the patient does not have TB before starting an immunosuppressive treatment for a non-infectious condition

## Nasal Polyps

- Nasal polyps: soft, painless, benign growths on the lining of the nasal passages or sinuses
- May develop as a consequence of repeated bouts of rhinitis, which may be allergic or infectious
- Allergic rhinitis is a Type I hypersensitivity reaction mediated by IgE

## Important Viruses: Herpesviruses

- ds DNA virus
- Latent infection – asymptomatic period following acute infection; no virus replication; hiding from the immune system
- Reactivation – transition from latent to active; lytic infection
  - Triggers: fever, microbial co-infection, UV light, stress, tissue injury, hyperthermia, hormonal imbalance, allogenic
stimulation, cytokines, cell differentiation, toxic stimuli
- Active infection – viral replication

## Approach to Fever in the Immunocompromised Host

- Different clinical conditions can impair different aspects of the immune system, which predisposes to different infections
  - Diabetes mellitus: low C4; decreased cytokine response after stimulation, chemotaxis, killing, and phagocytosis
  - Renal failure: uremia is associated with defective co-stimulatory function of antigen-presenting cells (hypothesized to
contribute to poor response to vaccines (Hepatitis B); decreased lymphocyte numbers in dialysis patients
- Immune deficiency can be a very dynamic process – it is all about timing
  - HIV risk: CD4 &lt; 50 (M. avium, CMV retinitis, lymphomas), CD4 > 500 (similar infections as non-infected individuals, TB)
  - High risk of invasive mold infections: chronic granulomatous disease, allogenic HSCT, lung/heart transplant, AML/MDS
  - Allogeneic stem cell transplant: patient receives healthy stem cells from a donor to replace their own stem cells that
have been conditioned by treatment with radiation or high doses of chemotherapy
    - Pre-engraftment (neutropenia), post-engraftment (NK cells recover), late phase (CD4 T cells recover)

- May not see fevers with cytokine-blocking medications: anakinra, rilonacept, tocilizumab, infliximab, golimumab, adalimumab
- When developing the DDx, think of the patient’s immune status – use the same differential + opportunistic infections + context
  - Presentation is commonly different between immunocompetent and immunocompromised patients
- Vaccination is imperative in immunocompromised patients
  - Live virus vaccines are generally unsafe
  - Give vaccines before immunosuppressive medications when possible
- Give an antimicrobial prophylaxis drug to patients with HIV or transplant recipients for infections that do not have a vaccine
