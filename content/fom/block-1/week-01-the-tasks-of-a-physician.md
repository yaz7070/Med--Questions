## MCC

- Sign: what health care practitioner identifies on PEx
- Symptom: what patient perceives as “not normal” for them

## Clinical Presentations

- Undifferentiated: symptom not specific to one body system – fatigue, malaise
- Single typical: symptom very specific to one body system – abdominal pain, diarrhea, knee swelling
- Multiple problems/multisystem: multiple symptoms across body systems, may be one or multiple causes – fever, cough
- Life threatening: ABCs
- Preventative care/health promotion: cancer and metabolic screening – pap test, fecal blood test, blood sugar, serum lipids

## Patient Centered Clinical Method

1. Exploring both disease and illness experiences – Hx, PEx, Ix, FIFE
2. Understanding the whole person – proximal (modifiable) and distal (non-modifiable) contexts
3. Finding common ground – problems, goals, roles, mutual decisions
4. Incorporating prevention and health promotion
5. Enhancing the patient-physician relationship
6. Being realistic

## FIFE

- Feelings – How does your disease/symptom make you feel?
- Ideas – What do you think is wrong?
- Function – How are your symptoms impacting your function/enjoyment of life?
- Expectations – What do you hope to get out of the medical visit?

## Choosing Wisely Canada

1. Consider the least invasive testing option first
2. Only order tests which will change patient management
3. Discuss and educate patients on whether tests are necessary
4. Seek clarification if you believe something was ordered unnecessarily
5. Do NOT recommend tests exclusively for the learning experience
6. Do NOT recommend tests in anticipation of preceptor preference

## ADL and IADL

- Activities of daily living (ADL): feeding, dressing, bathing, walking – deficits may indicate home healthcare/nursing facility
- Instrumental activities of daily living (IADL): cooking, cleaning, transportation, laundry, managing finances – improve QOL

## The 4 Ps

- Predisposing factors: put an individual at risk of developing a problem
- Precipitating factors: a specific event/trigger to the onset of the current problem
- Perpetuating factors: maintain the problem once it has become established
- Protective factors: strengths of the individual that reduce the severity of the problem

## WHO-6-Steps of Rational Prescribing

1. Patient’s problem – symptoms, diagnosis, patient characteristics
2. Therapeutic objective – curative, symptomatic, preventative
3. Treatment choice – contraindications, interactions, comedications
4. Start treatment – write Rx
5. Give patient information – instructions, warnings
6. Monitor treatment – continue, adjust, stop

## Levels of Prevention

- Health promotion/primordial prevention – entire population; prevent risk factors, lower average population risk
- Primary prevention – 1+ risk factors; manage risk factors, prevent development of disease
- Secondary prevention – limited disease; early diagnosis, prompt Tx, prevent disease progression/recurrence
- Tertiary prevention – symptomatic/advanced disease; reduce complications/disability

## Approaches to Disease Prevention and Control

- Clinical/individual-centered approach – causes of cases
- Public health/population-based approach – causes of disease incidence
- High-risk strategy – seeks to identify high-risk susceptible individuals
- Population strategy – seeks to control the determinants of incidence and lower mean level/shift the distribution of risk factors
- Prevention paradox: prevention that brings large benefits to the community offers little to each participating individual

## The Chronic Disease Continuum

Risk: probability that a specific action/exposure will give rise to a negative health outcome
Risk factor: individual characteristics or behaviours that increase the likelihood that a
person will suffer negative health outcomes
- Smoking, alcohol, diet, physical activity
Risk condition: chronic condition that contributes to risk of disease/disability/death
- Obesity, hypertension, pre-diabetes, high cholesterol/BP, metabolic syndrome
Multimorbidity: co-existence of 2+ chronic conditions
Co-morbidity: index disease holds priority over other co-occurring chronic diseases

## Ethical Theories

- Deontology/Kantianism – based on pure reason and duty; never treat others merely as a means to an end
- Utilitarianism – maximize utility and minimize bad
- Principlism/pluralistic theory – autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice
- Casuistry – bottom-up reasoning; inferring from similar past cases
- Narrative ethics – reflection and drawing broader lessons; personal identity and storytelling; highly contextual/subjective
- Feminist/communitarian ethics – critical of mainstream bioethics that neglect women’s issues/perspectives
- Pragmatism – looks for a 3rd “outside the box” solution
- Virtue ethics – focused on character of the individual; pursue eudaemonia; balance character traits between vices

## Practical Ethical Decision-Making

1. Recognition – gather and manage appropriate data considering the relevant values
2. Designation – clarify the goals and describe the ethical conflict
3. Evaluation – explain why this problem exists, distinguish the cause, and highlight ethical issues
4. Clarification – evaluate the feasible options and identify the implications
5. Determination – decide the “best” solution to the problem
6. Implementation – take action to implement with an established evaluation process

## Cultural Competence

- Cultural destructiveness: forced assimilation, subjugation, rights and privileges for dominant groups only
- Cultural incapacity: racism, maintain stereotypes, unfair hiring practices
- Cultural blindness: differences ignored, treat everyone the same, only meet needs of dominant groups
- Cultural pre-competence: expose cultural issues, are committed, assess needs of organization/individuals
- Cultural competence: recognize individual/cultural differences, seek advice from diverse groups, interact effectively
- Cultural proficiency: implement changes to improve services based upon cultural needs
- Cultural safety: fostering an environment that is spiritually, socially, and emotionally safe
- Cultural humility: recognition that our own perspective is not the only and not necessarily the “correct” perspective

## 10 Things to Know About the Health of Refugees and Migrants (WHO)

1. The health of refugees/migrants is important – basic human right
2. The numbers of migrants are often overestimated
3. Refugees/migrants are likely to be healthy in general
4. Refugees/migrants can face challenges in accessing health care – legal status, language barriers, discrimination
5. Refugees/migrants have a lower risk for all forms of cancer, except cervical cancer, but more diabetes than the host population
6. Breakdown in health systems in the country of origin
7. PTSD seems to be more prevalent among refugees and asylum seekers than the host population
8. Labour migrants constitute the largest group of migrants globally – more work-related injuries
9. Children without parents or a guardian are especially vulnerable
10. Making health systems refugee- and migrant-friendly

## History of Medicine

- Humoral theory: blood, yellow bile, black bile, phlegm
- Germ theory: identify the micro-organism, develop a laboratory diagnostic test, discover a chemotherapeutic agent
- Hippocrates – rejected religious/magical healing, introduced the medical practitioner as a diagnostician/therapist
- Galen – took clinical Hippocratic medicine and set it within a wider anatomy and physiology framework
- William Osler – emphasized the doctor-patient relationship and the importance of good bedside manner
