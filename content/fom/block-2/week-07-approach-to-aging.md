## Age-Related Definitions

- Agism: negative or positive stereotypes, prejudice, and/or discrimination on the grounds of a person’s age
- Healthy aging: a lifelong process of optimizing opportunities for improving and preserving health and physical, social, and
mental wellness, independence, quality of life, and enhancing successful life course transitions
  - Avoid smoking, Mediterranean diet/no snacking between meals, BMI = 24-30, 7-8 hours of sleep, limit alcohol
consumption, exercise regularly, be socially engaged
- Centenarian: a person who has lived to or past their 100th birthday – 1/10000, 5 females:1 male
  - Lean males, non-smoking Hx, females who naturally have a child after 40, first degree relative who achieved very old
age, low neuroticism/high extraversion, genes associated with longevity
- Super-centenarian: a person who has lived to or past their 110th birthday
- Life span: maximum obtainable age for a species defined by the oldest living individual – 122 years for humans
- Life expectancy: average number of years a person can expect to live based on year of birth and given age
- Normal aging: the process of growing old defined by gradual biological impairment
  - Cumulative over time, progressive in nature, universal to all in a species, intrinsic, deleterious
- Age-related changes: changes that occur universally in a species as the individual ages
- Usual aging: advanced age is often associated with increased susceptibility to and prevalence of certain diseases
- Disease-related changes: changes that occur in an individual due to disease and not merely growing older; not universal

## Mental Health Through Aging

- Mental disorders are just as common in the elderly as in younger age groups except for dementing disorders and delirium
- Prevalence of major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, substance abuse, and psychotic disorders is lower for those over 65
- Emotional control model: increased ability to inhibit negative emotional states and maintain positive emotional states
- Personality traits remain stable over the life span
- Passive coping style – reduce emotional distress rather than solve problem; modify goals, adapt to losses/stressors
- Older adults with strong social ties do better than those who are isolated or perceive that support is unavailable

| Factor | Examples |
|---|---|
| Biological | Physical health, disability/pain, comorbidity, cognition, absence of alcohol abuse/cigarette smoking, regular exercise |
| Psychological | Self-esteem/maintenance of identity, life satisfaction, sense of meaning/purpose in life, resilience, coping ability, absence of depression |
| Social | Relationships/social support, role changes, losses/grief, retirement, socioeconomic issues, living arrangement, spirituality, cultural aspects |

- Resilience: maintaining adaptive behaviour and positive emotions when dealing with stress, and recovery from adversity
- Selective optimization: an accommodative process of rescaling goals to match one’s limitations/demands of the environment

## Depression

- Older adults often deny depressed mood and minimize symptoms
- Cognitive impairment is often associated with depressive symptoms
- Stressful life events, such as recent bereavement, financial crises, or social isolation, may precipitate depression in the elderly
- Suicide is almost twice as frequent in older adults as in the general population and more lethal
  - Risk factors: white male, age 65+, living alone/poor supports, prior attempts, Hx of depression, psychotic features,
alcoholism, physical illness, disability, chronic pain, recent loss/bereavement
  - Severity of depression is the strongest predictor of the course of suicidal ideation
- Depression is a risk factor for diabetes; dementia and depression are risk factors for each other
- There are factors that make it challenging to recognize depression in older adults – different clinical features, misperception of
aging, concerns over stigma, coexisting medical illnesses, anxiety, cognitive impairment, cultural factors
- All individuals > 60 and those recently admitted to a LTC home should be screened for depression
  - PHQ-2 (cut off = 3), PHQ-9 (cut-off = 10), Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15, GDS-30; yes/no), Cornell Scale for
Depression in Dementia (19 items; 0 = absent, 1 = mild/intermittent, 2 = severe)
- Clinical presentation: affective symptoms, cognitive changes, no sadness, lack of feeling/emotion, somatic complaints,
irritability, social withdrawal/avoidance, loss of interest/pleasure in activities, unexplained functional impairment/decline
  - SIG-E-CAPS: sleep, interest, guilt, energy, concentration, appetite, psychomotor, suicidal ideation

## MoCA Scoring

1. Alternative trail making: any error = 0, correct drawing = 1
2. Visuoconstructional skills (cube): 3D, all lines drawn, no lines added, lines relatively parallel with similar length = 1
3. Visuoconstructional skills (clock): contour = 1, numbers = 1, hands = 1
4. Naming (animals): camel/dromedary = 1, lion = 1, rhinoceros/rhino = 1
5. Memory: no points for trials 1 and 2
6. Attention (forward digit span, backward digit span): 2-1-8-5-4 = 1, 2-4-7 = 1
Vigilance (tapping for A): zero/one error = 1
Serial 7’s: no correct subtractions = 0, 1 correct subtraction = 1, 2/3 correct subtractions = 2, 4/5 correct subtractions = 3
7. Sentence repetition: exact repetition of sentence = 2
8. Verbal fluency: 11 words or more in 60 sec = 1
9. Abstraction: means of transportation/travelling/trips = 1, measuring instruments/used to measure = 1
10. Delayed recall: each word recalled freely without any cues = 5
11. Orientation (year, month, exact date, day of the week, name of the place, city): correct answer = 6

## Dementia

- Alzheimer’s, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal, mixed

| Stage | Risk Factors |  |
|---|---|---|
| Early life | Less education | 40% potentially modifiable |
| Mid life | Hearing loss, traumatic brain injury, hypertension, alcohol > 21 units per week, obesity | 60% risk unknown |
| Later life | Smoking, depression, social isolation, physical inactivity, air pollution, diabetes |  |

- DDx for cognitive changes: delirium, depression, drugs (anticholinergic medications, sedatives, alcohol), metabolic abnormalities
(vitamin B12 deficiency, liver/thyroid dysfunction)
- Ix to rule out mimickers: CBC, TSH, electrolytes, calcium (+ albumin), glucose, vitamin B12 level
- Neuroimaging (BRAIN) – MRI is the best test to identify vascular changes, but CT done first
  - Bleeding risk - head trauma, anticoagulation/bleeding disorder
  - Abnormal presentation – age &lt; 60, rapid/unexplained decline over 1-2 months, short duration of dementia (&lt; 2 years)
  - Intracranial lesion – Hx of cancer, unexplained focal neurological sign/symptom, unusual/atypical cognitive
presentation, significant vascular risk factors for stroke
  - Normal pressure hydrocephalus – gait disturbance, urinary incontinence
- Safety issues: driving (MVCs), wandering (door alarms, GPS devices in shoes, ID bracelets), home safety (fires, falls), managing
meals/medications/finances/self-care, vulnerability to crime/financial fraud/elder abuse
- Future planning: ADs, POA documents, applying for LTC (lists are often 2-3 years long)
- Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) – agitation, aggression, hallucinations, delusions
  - Non-pharmacologic management – distraction (music therapy, activities), look for triggers (pain, medications,
constipation, urinary retention)
  - DICE: describe symptoms, investigate causes, create a Tx plan, evaluate whether strategies effective
  - Pharmacologic management – SSRIs (citalopram, escitalopram), antipsychotics (last resort)

## Frailty and Falls

- Frailty: a complex biologic syndrome of decreased reserve and resistance to stressors and increased vulnerability to adverse
outcomes; not synonymous with comorbidity, disability, or aging
- Clinical Frailty Scale – degree of frailty corresponds to the degree of dementia

| Score | Clinical |  |
|---|---|---|
| 1. | Very fit | Robust, active, energetic, and motivated; exercise regularly; among the fittest for their age |
| 2. | Well | No active disease symptoms, but are less fit than category 1; exercise or are very active occasionally (seasonally) |
| 3. | Managing well | Medical problems are well controlled, but are not regularly active beyond routine walking |
| 4. | Vulnerable | While not dependent on others for daily help, often symptoms limit activities; being “slowed up”/tired during the day |
| 5. | Mildly frail | More evident slowing and need help in high order IADLs; impaired shopping/walking alone, meal preparation, housework |
| 6. | Moderately frail | Need help with all outside activities and housekeeping; problems with stairs, need help with bathing, may need help dressing |
| 7. | Severely frail | Completely dependent for personal care; stable and not at high risk of dying within 6 months |
| 8. | Very severely frail | Completely dependent, approaching end of life; typically, could not recover even from a minor illness |
| 9. | Terminally ill | Approaching end of life, with a life expectancy &lt; 6 months, who are not otherwise evidently frail |

- Risk factors: medical illnesses (stroke, diabetes, arthritis, COPD, cognitive impairment), female gender, low socioeconomic
status, depression, disability requiring assistance with ADLs
- Signs: sarcopenia, osteopenia, balance/gait problems, deconditioning, malnutrition, slow gait speed
- Symptoms: weakness, weight loss, fatigue, inactivity, decreased food intake/anorexia
- Consequences: increased risk of falls, hospitalization, longer lengths of stay, worse outcomes in hospital, dependency, death
- Non-pharmacologic prevention: exercise, prevention of immobility, Mediterranean diet, nutritional supplementation with
protein, medical assessment through a comprehensive geriatric Ax

## Falls

- Fall: an event which results in a person coming to rest inadvertently on the ground or floor
- Leading cause of injuries in older adults; 5th leading cause of death in adults > 65 years
- Associated with injuries (fractures, brain injuries, bruises), disability (fear of falling, loss of mobility), LTC admission, longer
hospital stays, death
- Falls are almost always multifactorial – never say “mechanical fall” (trip, accident)
- Intrinsic causes: poor balance, weakness, chronic illness, visual impairment, cognitive impairment
- Extrinsic causes: medications, alcohol, poor lighting, loose carpets, uneven ground, stairs, pets, weather

International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health
- Disability: interaction between individuals with a health condition, and personal and
environmental factors
- Impairments: problems in body function or alterations in body structure – paralysis
- Activity limitations: difficulties in executing activities – walking, eating
- Participation restrictions: problems with involvement in any area of life – discrimination in
employment or transportation

## Geriatric Choosing Wisely Recommendations

1. Don’t use antimicrobials to treat bacteriuria in older adults unless specific urinary tract symptoms are present
2. Don’t use benzodiazepines or other sedative-hypnotics in older adults as first choice for insomnia, agitation, or delirium
3. Don’t recommend percutaneous feeding tubes in patients with advanced dementia; instead offer oral feeding
4. Don’t use antipsychotics as first choice to treat behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia
5. Avoid using medication known to cause hypoglycemia to achieve hemoglobin A1c &lt; 7.5% in many adults aged 65 and older;
moderate control is generally better

## Organization of the Motor System

- Motor cortex in the frontal lobe plans movement on the contralateral side of the brain to the eventual movement
- Motor neuron axons descend through white matter tracts (internal capsule) à brainstem à decussate in the pyramids of the
medulla à descend in spinal cord à VGH à spinal nerve à peripheral nerve à neuromuscular junction à muscle
- Input from the cerebellum (improves accuracy) and the basal ganglia (control/integration/timing) loops back to the motor
cortex to refine movement in feedback loops
- Lesions in the cerebellum do NOT cause weakness, but cause difficulties with coordination
- Dysfunction of the basal ganglia do NOT cause weakness, but cause difficulty initiating movements, slow movements, and
rigidity (Parkinsonism), or excessive movements (chorea, dystonia, athetosis, hemiballismus)
