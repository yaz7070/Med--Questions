## Pharmacology Definitions

- Pharmacology: field of science concerned with the biology, chemistry, and physiology of drug disposition, effects, and toxicity
- Drug: a medicine or other substance which has a physiological effect when introduced into the body; used in the diagnosis,
treatment, or prevention of a disease or as a component of a medication
- Clinical pharmacology: the study of drug action in man, providing the scientific basis for rational, safe, and effective prescribing
- Therapeutics: field of medicine concerned with the use of treatments to cure, control, or prevent disease
- Prescribing: a written order, which includes detailed instructions of what medicine should be given to whom, in what
formulation and dose, by what route, when, how frequently, and for how long
- Pharmacodynamics: the effects of a drug on the body; how drugs work
- Pharmacokinetics: the quantitative description of drug concentrations in the body over time; how the body deals with drugs

## Pharmacological Targets

- Receptors – ligand-gated ion channels, G protein-coupled receptors, enzyme-linked receptors, intracellular receptors
- Enzymes – reductases, transferases, lyases, hydrolases
- Endogenous molecules – HCl, RANKL, nucleic acids, structural proteins
- Transport proteins – SGLT2 transporter, Na+/K+ ATPase pump

## Receptors

- Receptor: a macromolecule to which a drug binds to produce a measurable effect
- The coupling between the ligand-receptor complex to its downstream effectors triggers a cascade of signalling events
- D-R interaction – quantitative relation between dose and effect; selectivity of drug action
  - Orthosteric site = main binding site of the agonist; allosteric sites = other binding sites
- Exist in an active and inactive state – magnitude of the biological effect ∝ fraction of receptors in the active state
- Termination – dissociation/destruction of the D-R complex, desensitization of the receptors (drug tolerance, tachyphylaxis)

## Dose-Response Curve

- Closely related to the drug-receptor binding relationship
- Doubling the dose ≠ doubling the effect
- Drugs have different dose-response profiles
- Individual variability – monitor the patient’s therapeutic response

## Potency and Efficacy of Drugs

- Efficacy (Emax): the maximal response produced by a drug
  - As receptors become occupied, there is an increase in response up to Emax
  - Emax may occur when all receptors are bound or only a fraction are occupied
- Potency (EC50/ED50): concentration of dose required to produce 50% of the Emax
  - Lower EC50 = more potent drug
- Competitive antagonists decrease the potency of agonists, but do not change the efficacy
- Non-competitive antagonists decrease the efficacy of agonists, but do not change the potency

## Drug Absorption

- Absorption: the movement of a drug from its site of administration to the systemic circulation
  - Rate of absorption affects onset of action – physiochemical properties/formulations (dissolution, pH, lipid solubility),
blood flow, activity of drug transport proteins (OATs, P-glycoprotein), route of administration (therapeutic goal)
  - Amount of drug absorbed determines magnitude of effect
- Bioavailability: the proportion of administered drug (parent drug) that reaches the systemic circulation in an unaltered form
  - Consider absorption (intrinsic properties of the drug, DDI, DFI) and first-pass metabolism effect

## Drug Distribution

- Distribution: the movement of a drug from the main circulation into its sites of action
- Medication is first carried to highly vascularized tissues – brain, heart, liver, kidneys à skin, muscle, fat, bone
- Blood brain barrier (BBB): a highly selective semipermeable barrier between the blood circulation and the brain/extracellular
fluid around the brain that restricts access of many molecules to the brain – carrier-mediated transport, lipid-soluble substances
- Volume of distribution (Vd): the extent to which a drug partitions between the plasma and tissue compartments
- Factors that affect distribution: blood flow, capillary permeability, lipid solubility, disease state (heart/liver/kidney failure), age
(↑ body fat, ↓ plasma volume, ↓ total body water, ↓ extracellular body fluid)

## Drug Metabolism

- Metabolism: the chemical modification of a drug by a biological system
- Sites of metabolism: liver (primary site), intestine (enterocytes), stomach (alcohol), kidney, intestinal bacterial
- Metabolism can activate drugs (prodrug), inactivate drugs, increase/decrease toxicity, increase drug effectiveness

- Phase I – Cytochrome P450 system mediates most oxidation/reduction reactions; exhibit significant genetic variability
  - CYP3A4/5 = 30.2%; CYP2D6 = 20%; CYP2C9 = 12.8%; CYP1A2 = 8.9%
- Phase II – transferases add hydrophilic groups; glucuronidation, acetylation, sulfation, glutathione/thiol combination
- Enzyme induction = LOWER drug levels; enzyme inhibition = HIGHER drug levels; opposite for prodrugs
- Increased metabolic rate decreases drug duration/intensity; decreased metabolic rate increases drug duration/intensity
- Factors that affect metabolism: DDI (enzyme induction/inhibition), disease state (liver/kidney disease, infection, inflammatory
disease), age, gender, genetic polymorphisms, blood flow

## Drug Excretion

- Excretion: the process of eliminating the parent drug and its metabolites from the body
1. Renal drug excretion: glomerular filtration à passive tubular reabsorption à active tubular secretion à excretion
2. Hepatic metabolism – CYP enzymes
3. Biliary excretion: liver à gallbladder à bile duct à duodenum à excretion in feces OR deconjugated and reabsorbed via
hepatic portal vein (enterohepatic recycling); high molecular weight, conjugated drugs, amphipathic molecules
- Other routes of excretion: lungs (respiration), hair follicles (sweat), salivary glands, breast milk
- Clearance (CL): rate at which a drug is removed from plasma divided by the concentration of that drug in the plasma (mL/min)
- Factors that affect excretion: drug related factors (water soluble), blood flow, renal function (GFR), hepatic function, disease
state, age (↓ renal plasma flow, ↓ glomerular filtration rate, ↓ number of functional glomeruli)

## Clinical Pharmacology

- Graded dose-response curve = one patient
- Quantal dose-response curve = population of patients; all/none; therapeutic effect (ED50), toxic effect (TD50), lethal effect (LD50)
  - Can extract the % of individuals who exhibit the effect plotted as a function of log dose; standard dose = starting point
- Half-life (t1/2): the time for the blood concentration of a drug to decline by one-half; dose is usually removed after 3-5 half-lives
  - Determines the duration of drug action and the frequency a drug needs to be given to maintain drug effects
  - Larger volume of distribution = longer t1/2; higher clearance = shorter t1/2; independent of dose
- Steady state concentration (Css): the plasma drug concentration at which the rate of drug delivery equals the rate of elimination
  - Takes 3-5 half-lives for most drugs to reach the steady-state; independent of dose
  - For every dosing regimen with the same daily dose rate, Css is the same, but the peak and trough fluctuation is different
- Maintenance dose: maintains a peak plasma concentration below the toxic concentration and a trough plasma concentration
above the minimally effective dose
- Loading dose: larger than the maintenance dose and allows for attainment of therapeutic concentrations more rapidly
- Therapeutic window: represents the range between the minimum toxic dose and the minimum therapeutic/effective dose
  - ED50 – monitor for efficacy and adverse drug reactions; use as a starting dose when there is a wide therapeutic window;
titrate dose when there is a narrow therapeutic window
  - Dosing regimens should be designed to avoid toxicity or lack of efficacy and when possible, to provide convenient
intervals to promote patient compliance

## Adverse Drug Reactions and Adverse Drug Events

- Adverse drug reaction (ADR): any noxious, unintended, and undesired effect of a drug, which occurs at doses used in humans
for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy; something bad happens even though you give the right drug, right dose, and right route

| Immunologic ADRs | Antibody-mediated | Pure T-cell mediated |
|---|---|---|
| Non-immunologic ADRs | Predictable | Pharmacological side effect – dry mouth from antihistamines |

Secondary pharmacologic side effect – thrush while taking antibiotics
Drug toxicity – hepatotoxicity from methotrexate
DDIs – seizure from theophylline while taking erythromycin
Drug overdose – seizure from excessive lidocaine (Xylocaine)
Unpredictable · Pseudoallergic – anaphylactoid reaction after radiocontrast media
Idiosyncratic – hemolytic anemia in a patient with G6PD deficiency after primaquine therapy
Intolerance – tinnitus after a single, small dose of aspirin

- Adverse drug event (ADE): an injury resulting from medical intervention related to a drug; includes errors in administration
  - Error: something bad happens because someone/something made a mistake
  - Preventable adverse event: something bad happens that could have been avoided
  - Near miss: something bad almost happened
Drug Interaction
Drug-drug interaction (DDI) · Induce drug metabolism – increase clearance of other drugs and make them less effective
Inhibit drug metabolism/excretion – decrease clearance of other drugs and make them more potent/potentially more toxic
PK interactions involve changes in the disposition of a drug
Alteration in bioavailability – chemical interactions, alteration in gastric pH (ketoconazole, digoxin), alteration in GI motility
Alteration in drug elimination is the most common cause of adverse drug interaction
PD interactions involve drugs with complementary or antagonistic therapeutic effects
Drug-food interaction (DFI) · Grapefruit juice inhibits the clearance of some statins – a constituent of grapefruit destroys CYP3A4; effects can last for 3 days
after the last serving; can lead to statin-induced myopathy and enhance the hypotensive effects of calcium channel blockers
Leafy green vegetables can decrease the efficacy of warfarin due to their Vitamin K content
Drug-disease interaction · Viral infections such as influenza can alter drug metabolism, typically by inhibition – can lead to toxicity

## Contraindications and Reporting

- Indication: a disorder for which a drug is effective and should be used
- Contraindication: a disorder for which a drug is harmful and should not be used
- When a patient is on therapy, (1) be aware of the possibility of ADEs, (2) monitor for ADEs, (3) understand the course of disease
- Inform patient/family/other health care providers about the ADE, and Health Canada for new drugs/unusual adverse events

## Therapeutics in Older Adults

- Polypharmacy: the use of multiple medications by a patient; minimum 5-10 medications
  - Increase in medication = increase in cost, ADRs, poor adherence, DDIs, risk of hospitalization
- Factors leading to ADRs: multimorbidity, changes in PK/PD with aging, prescribing cascades, heterogeneity, lack of inclusion in
clinical research, fragmentation/specialization of healthcare, frequent transitions in care, advances in healthcare/guidelines
- Review medications at least annually – have patients bring in bottles, at transitions of care, ask about side effects/benefits, look
for duplication/cascades, eliminate unnecessary medications, simplify dosing regimens
- When starting a new medication: Is this medication necessary? Does it align with the patient’s frailty status and goals? Is the
medication covered by Ontario Drug Benefits? What are the therapeutic endpoints? Do the benefits outweigh the risks? Are you
treating effects of another drug? Could one drug be used to treat two conditions? Could it interact with diseases or other drugs?
Does the patient know what it’s for and what to watch out for?

## Approach to Therapeutics

- Therapeutic success – patient narrative (symptom management), signs (PEx), bloodwork, imaging, Ax of end-organ function
- Adverse effects – therapeutic drug monitoring, physiological response, biomarkers, imaging
Factors to Help Make Prescribing Decisions (STEPS)
Safety · FHx of ADRs, review medication adherence/ADRs with each office visit, screen for clinical/biochemical signs of toxicity, therapeutic drug
monitoring, patient information – ability to adhere to oral vs. intramuscular vs. subcutaneous medications
Tolerability · Assess patient’s tolerance of medication(s), impact on quality of life
Effectiveness What is the evidence that a drug is likely to make a patient live longer compared with available alternatives?

| Price | Clarify patient’s medical insurance coverage, affordable medications – brand name vs. generics |
|---|---|
| Simplicity | Making a drug regimen as simple as possible is an important step in achieving adherence – q.d. vs b.i.d. |

- Instructions: dose, ingest +/- food, frequency of ingestions, total duration of medication course, contact number if questions
- Information/warnings: benefits (purpose), side effects (common, dangerous), risk of ADRs/DDIs/DFIs/drug-disease interactions
- Deprescribing: the process of withdrawal of an inappropriate medication, supervised by a healthcare professional with the goal
of managing polypharmacy and improving outcomes
  - Target populations: changes to clinical practice guidelines, new comorbidities, polypharmacy, palliative care
  - Barriers: awareness, inertia, self-efficacy, feasibility
- General practitioners, nurse practitioners, medical specialists, and pharmacists can collect/distribute information with every
patient encounter – new diagnosis, iterative office visits, hospital discharge, visits for out-of-hospital bloodwork/pharmacy

## Canadian Health Care System and Drug Therapy

- Health care was designated to be a provincial responsibility at the time of Confederation (not costly, few drugs), but over time,
it has expanded to include universal coverage of basic health services and hospital care (40-50% of provincial budgets)
- 10 provincial systems, 3 territorial systems, small federal system (Canadian Armed Forces, Her Majesty’s prisons)

- Drugs are prescribed by physicians (provincial); paid for by patients/private plans/governments (federal, provincial); approved,
regulated, and monitored by the federal government; and sold by industry (federal regulation, provincial incorporation)
- Drug formulary: a list of medications that are approved for use in a hospital or that are provided by a drug plan
  - All provinces and territories have drug plans – number/type of drugs and eligibility varies considerably

## Drug Utilization in Canada

- Total spending on drugs in Canada is \$40 billion/year – 16% of health care costs; \$1100/person; 85% on prescription drugs
- 50% of drug costs are paid by public expenditure, 25% by private drug plans, and 25% by individuals
- Patients ≤ 18 on average receive 4 prescriptions/year; number of drugs prescribed increases with age
- Top drug classes prescribed: cardiovascular, psychotherapeutics, gastrointestinal/genitourinary
- Percentage of drug sales: antineoplastic and immunomodulating agents, general anti-infectives, alimentary tract/metabolism
- Major cost drivers are age high-cost drugs (rheumatoid arthritis/Chron’s disease, Hepatitis C, age-related macular degeneration)
- OECD average pharmaceutical spending = \$719/year per capita; Canada = \$1043/year and ranks \#3 after USA and Switzerland
- OCED average life expectancy = 80.6 years; Canada = 81.7, but ranks \#13
- Barriers to optimal drug therapy: finances, SDH, geography, health care system challenges/accessibility
